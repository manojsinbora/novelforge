Chapter 2: The Bleeding Avenues


The outside world looked like a clearance sale at an explosives factory.


It had been roughly twenty-five minutes since the first tremor cracked the library basement, and the city of Oakhaven had already abandoned all pretense of civilized behavior.


Arthur stepped out of the heavy iron fire door into the back alley behind Section 14. The afternoon sky was the color of a bruised plum, streaked with veins of luminous violet. Down at the end of the alley, where the brick passage opened onto Commerce Avenue, cars were piled three-deep against a shattered bronze fountain. A broken fire hydrant was shooting a geyser of white water forty feet into the air, creating a lovely, shimmering rainbow directly above an overturned bakery delivery van.


Arthur adjusted his brass-rimmed respirator, pulled out his pocket watch, and clicked the stem.


2:21 PM.


"Four miles to St. Jude Clinic," he calculated under his breath, his voice vibrating with a metallic rasp behind the charcoal filter. "Average walking speed: three point five miles per hour. That’s sixty-eight minutes on clear pavement. Factoring in street fires, civilian panic, and municipal structural collapse... call it ninety minutes. Assuming I do not get eaten."


He checked the leather straps of his rucksack, tapped his drop-forged pipe wrench against his thigh, and stepped into the alley.


Two steps later, he paused.


A small cardboard box of library catalog cards was lying in a puddle near the dumpster. Arthur frowned, reached down, picked it up, set it carefully on top of a dry recycling bin, and smoothed the corners.


"Rain damage on index stock," he muttered, shaking his head. "Disgraceful."


Satisfied that the catalog cards had a fighting chance at survival, he continued toward Commerce Avenue.


The street was utter madness.


People were fleeing in every direction, carrying the most bizarre assortments of belongings. One man in a torn business suit sprinted past clutching a toaster oven and three rolls of bubble wrap. Across the street, two university students were arguing violently over who got to carry a sixty-five-inch flat-screen television through four feet of toxic purple mist.


Arthur watched them with detached fascination. "Yes, of course. When ancient subterranean horrors awake to reclaim the surface world, high-definition resolution is the top priority."


He kept strictly to the sidewalks, slipping beneath shop awnings and staying close to brick walls. The center of the street was where cars crashed, flying glass landed, and panicked mobs trampled each other. Every old municipal disaster manual from the 1910s made that abundantly clear.


The purple fog was heavier out here on the avenue. It clung to the asphalt like dry ice, swirling around the wheels of abandoned sedans and creeping through the storm drains. The sweet, rotten scent of wild jasmine was so potent that even through his charcoal filter, Arthur could taste a faint hint of copper on the back of his tongue.


And the mist wasn't just sitting there. It was changing things.


Fifty yards ahead, a flock of city pigeons was pecking at an overturned bag of pretzel dough. As the purple vapor coiled around their wings, the birds convulsed. Feathers moulted in wet clumps, replaced by jagged quill-spikes of dark bone. Their beaks elongated into jagged shears of yellow keratin, and their eyes turned milky white, snapping their heads left and right with unnatural, twitching speed.


Arthur stopped behind a mailbox, pulled out a small leather-bound notebook and a stub of pencil, and made a quick note:


Avian mutation timeline: Under three minutes of respiratory exposure. Symptoms: feather loss, bone-spike growth, blindness accompanied by rapid auditory twitching. Note to self: do not feed the pigeons.


He slipped the notebook back into his pocket. He loved empirical data. It was the only thing that kept the world from feeling completely unhinged.


Ahead, on the corner of 5th and Commerce, a neon sign flickered through the purple haze: \1.


The front display window had already been shattered, but the heavy steel interior security gate was half-open.


Arthur’s eyes lit up.


To an ordinary person, a hardware store during an apocalypse was a place to grab a hatchet or a flashlight. To Arthur Vance, it was an alchemical forge disguised as a retail establishment. A fourteen-inch pipe wrench was a fine defensive tool against an eight-foot mutant security guard in a tight basement, but across four miles of open city streets? He needed range, he needed voltage, and he needed a lot more leverage.


He ducked beneath the shattered glass of the entrance and slipped inside.


The store smelled pleasantly of motor oil, galvanized steel, and cedar sawdust. An overhead fluorescent bulb buzzed in rhythmic death throes.


Behind the checkout counter, a middle-aged clerk wearing a bright green apron was curled into a trembling ball inside the cash register well, clutching a plastic staple gun as if it were a high-caliber rifle.


Arthur approached the counter, cleared his throat, and politely tapped the little silver service bell on the desk.


Ding.


The clerk shrieked, popping his head up with wild eyes, brandishing the staple gun. "Stay back! I’m armed! I’ll staple your forehead!"


"Good afternoon," Arthur said pleasantly, his voice muffled behind the brass respirator. "I’m borrowing some inventory. Don't mind me."


"Are—are you a looter?" the clerk squeaked, staring at Arthur's gray librarian smock, wire-rimmed glasses, and heavy charcoal mask.


"Looting is an uncoordinated civilian crime driven by opportunism," Arthur replied with mild offense. "I am requisitioning supplies for an emergency family rescue. Furthermore, I intend to pay."


Leaving the stunned clerk gaping, Arthur moved through the aisles with laser focus.


His eyes swept the shelves, his brain cross-referencing materials like an index catalog:


Aisle 3: High-tension steel garage door springs. \1

Aisle 5: Fifty feet of braided aircraft cable, five-hundred-pound test weight. \1

Aisle 8: A box of high-voltage industrial ignition capacitors and a twelve-volt lithium battery pack designed for cordless power drills. \1

Aisle 12: A roll of reinforced rubber grip tape and a box of hardened steel masonry chisels.


Arthur carried the components to the customer workbench in the tool-sharpening bay at the back of the store.


He checked his pocket watch. \1


Moving with practiced ease, Arthur set his fourteen-inch pipe wrench on the workbench vice.


First, he took a five-inch steel masonry chisel, ground the flat end to match the jaw slot of the wrench, and clamped it firmly into place with a high-tensile steel bracket. Now the wrench head had a chisel-edge spike extending from its crown—perfect for cracking armored chitin without blunting the grip teeth.


Next came the electronics. He stripped the lead wires on two ignition capacitors, bound them directly to the lithium drill battery with rubber electrical tape, and ran copper grounding leads along the handle beneath the insulated grip. He wired a small push-button momentary switch right next to where his thumb rested.


When he tapped the switch, a vicious blue electric arc snapped across the chisel tip with a sharp \1.


Roughly fifteen thousand volts,* Arthur estimated, nodding with deep professional satisfaction. *Not enough amperage to kill an elephant, but certainly enough to overload the peripheral nervous system of an overgrown reptile.


He wrapped the handle in thick layers of vulcanized rubber to prevent himself from getting cooked, slotted the battery into the base of the handle, and weighed the modified weapon in his hand.


It was heavier now, perfectly balanced toward the head, dangerous, and utterly absurd.


He dubbed it \1.


Arthur walked back to the front counter, pulled a twenty-dollar bill and a handful of silver coins from his vest pocket, and placed them neatly on the glass beside the service bell.


"Ten dollars for the capacitors, six for the cable, and four for the battery pack," Arthur said. "Keep the change."


The clerk looked at the money, then looked at the terrifying electrified war-hammer in Arthur's hand, and swallowed hard. "You... you actually paid."


"I am an archivist, sir," Arthur said coldly. "We have standards. Have a pleasant afternoon, and consider boarding up your rear door. Mutants have poor eyesight but excellent hearing."


Arthur ducked back out onto Commerce Avenue.


He hadn't taken twenty paces toward the bus terminal when the screams started up again.


Only this time, they weren't distant. They were fifty feet ahead, right in the center of the avenue intersection.


A municipal city bus had crashed sideways across the crosswalk, its front windshield spider-webbed with cracks. Inside, a dozen terrified passengers were banging desperately against the tinted safety glass, their faces twisted in horror.


Surrounding the bus was a pack of three mutated street hounds.


They used to be stray pit bulls and rottweilers, but the purple mist had completely remade them. Their fur had sloughed away, exposing raw, knotted red muscle covered in greasy gray scales. Their skulls had broadened, their snouts split open like blooming flowers lined with jagged shark teeth. They were blind—milky white fluid oozed from their empty eye sockets—sniffing the air with wet, guttural snaps of their jaws.


And standing atop an overturned newspaper kiosk between the beasts and the bus was a man shouting at the top of his lungs.


The man was broad-shouldered, wearing a neon yellow gym tank top, red track pants, and a backwards baseball cap. In his hands, he gripped an aluminum softball bat.


"Back off, you ugly mutts!" the man roared, flexing his massive biceps and swinging the bat through the air with loud swooshes. "You picked the wrong corner! I’m Chadwick Briggs! Golden Gloves regional semi-finalist! My inner dragon has awakened! Come get some!"


The lead hound—a massive beast the size of a heifer—turned its head toward the sound of Chadwick’s shouting.


Arthur stopped behind a lamppost, rubbing his forehead with two fingers. "Why do people insist on delivering theatrical monologues to blind apex predators?"


The hound crouched, its hindquarters bunching with grotesque power.


Chadwick raised the aluminum bat like a samurai, took a dramatic warrior stance, and charged forward with a deafening war cry: "DRAGON FIST OVERDRIVE—"


CLANG.


Chadwick swung with all his might. The bat struck the lead hound squarely across the shoulder.


The aluminum bat bent at a ninety-degree angle like a wet noodle.


The hound didn't even flinch. It simply swiped one heavy, clawed paw across Chadwick’s chest.


"Aaargh!" Chadwick screamed, flying backward through the air like a discarded ragdoll. He crashed squarely into a row of three green municipal dumpsters, vanished inside, and was promptly buried beneath an avalanche of cardboard coffee cups, banana peels, and discarded newspaper.


The passengers inside the bus shrieked in despair.


The lead hound shook its head, smelled the blood on its claws, and turned toward the thin metal door of the bus, rearing up to smash the aluminum panels.


Arthur clicked his stopwatch. \1


He took a quiet breath, checked the grip on \1, and picked up an empty soda can from the sidewalk.


With a smooth flick of his wrist, he lobbed the soda can across the street.


Tink-tink-tink.


The can rattled against a brass fire hydrant thirty feet to the hound's left.


Instantly, the beast’s ears swiveled. It let out a snarling hiss, its blind head snapping toward the sound of the metallic rattle. It turned its flank toward Arthur, exposing the pale, unarmored skin behind its front leg where the rib cage met the spine.


Arthur didn't yell a battle cry. Yelling was for people who wanted to give away their position.


He sprinted forward on silent rubber soles, closed the ten-yard gap in two seconds flat, and slid across the wet asphalt beneath the creature's blind arc.


As he slid, Arthur drove the chisel spike of \1 straight up into the nerve cluster behind the beast's left shoulder.


THWACK.


The steel chisel bit two inches into flesh.


Simultaneously, Arthur depressed the thumb switch.


CRACKLE-SNAP!


A blinding discharge of fifteen thousand volts flooded directly into the creature's spinal cord.


The hound didn't roar. It didn't even twitch. The massive shock instantly scrambled its motor synapses. Its entire nervous system seized in an involuntary tetanic spasm, locking every muscle in its body as hard as cured concrete.


THUMP.


The beast collapsed sideways like a felled redwood tree, completely paralyzed and comatose.


The second hound, startled by the ozone stench and the sudden thud of its pack leader, whipped around and lunged blindly toward Arthur, jaws snapping.


Arthur didn't even stand up. He grabbed the coil of high-tension aircraft cable from his belt, whipped the weighted loop around the beast’s incoming forelegs, and dropped his weight, anchoring the cable against the heavy cast-iron base of an old street mailbox.


The charging hound hit the cable at full speed.


YELP.


Its front legs were violently yanked out from under it. Momentum carried its two-hundred-pound body flying over Arthur’s head, where it slammed skull-first into the reinforced steel frame of a municipal utility box with the force of a bowling ball.


The box caved in. Sparks flew. The beast went limp, thoroughly knocked out cold.


The third hound, suddenly finding itself alone in an alley that smelled like burnt ozone, lightning, and broken bones, whined softly. It backed up two paces, turned its tail between its scaled legs, and bolted down the street as fast as its claws could carry it.


Arthur stood up smoothly, dusted the gray lime from his knees, and let go of the thumb switch.


"High-voltage electrical shock to the upper spinal ganglia," he observed quietly. "Fascinating. The neuromuscular conduction in mutated canines mirrors that of standard \1. That will be useful later."


He was just about to wipe the chisel clean on a paper flyer when the lids of the municipal dumpsters flew open with a tremendous clatter.


BANG.


Chadwick Briggs exploded out of the garbage cans like a man resurrected from the grave. He was covered from head to toe in soggy lettuce leaves, coffee grounds, and a tangled mess of plastic six-pack rings, clutching the snapped handle of his bent aluminum bat.


"BEHOLD MY WRATH!" Chadwick bellowed at the top of his lungs, chest heaving, his eyes wild and completely disoriented from the concussion. "I HAVE CONSUMED THE POWER OF THE EARTH! DRAGON ROAR!"


He kicked open the dumpster door, stumbled out onto the street, and raised his bent bat triumphantly into the air.


At that exact moment, the doors of the municipal bus finally forced open.


Twelve trapped passengers poured out onto the street, sobbing, cheering, and falling into each other’s arms.


Then their eyes landed on Chadwick.


He was standing in the center of the road, covered in garbage, striking a legendary warrior pose with his broken bat, while directly beneath him lay two monstrous, scaled demon hounds—one smoking with blue electric sparks, the other wedged into a crushed utility box.


"He... he did it!" a woman screamed in absolute adoration. "The man from the dumpster! He summoned the thunder!"


"Chadwick!" an old man shouted, wiping tears from his cheeks. "He took the beast's blow, absorbed the kinetic energy into the trash realm, and struck them down with heavenly lightning! Look at the smoke rising from the demon's hide!"


"A Hero of the Awakening!" a student cheered, snapping photos on his cracked smartphone. "Chadwick the Trash Dragon! The Immortal of Commerce Avenue!"


Chadwick froze.


He looked down at his bent bat. He looked at the smoking, electrified carcass of the monster. He looked at the cheering crowd worshiping his name.


A slow, massive, entirely unearned grin spread across Chadwick's face.


"Ahem," Chadwick coughed, brushing a piece of rotten celery off his shoulder and flexing his chest. "Yes! Exactly as I calculated! The Way of the Trash Dragon requires patience and immense sacrifice! Citizens, you are safe under my aegis!"


Arthur was standing three feet away, holding \1 by his side.


A young woman rushed past Arthur, shoving him slightly with her shoulder as she hurried toward Chadwick with an unopened bottle of sparkling mineral water.


"Excuse me, young man," an elderly lady with a floral parasol said, tapping Arthur’s smock impatiently. "Don't just stand there daydreaming in your work smock! Go help that brave hero carry his trophies! And see if you can sweep up this broken glass before someone gets hurt!"


Arthur stood perfectly motionless.


Behind his wire-rimmed spectacles, his left eye gave two sharp, rapid twitches.


He looked at the smoking battery pack wired to his wrench. He looked at the aircraft cable neatly secured to the mailbox. He looked at Chadwick, who was currently signing an autograph on an empty coffee cup with a borrowed lipstick.


"...The Trash Dragon," Arthur whispered into his respirator. "He was in a garbage bin for twelve seconds. He smelled like expired mayonnaise. And they think he summoned localized lightning."


Arthur closed his eyes, counted slowly to three, and exhaled through the charcoal filter.


"I need to leave this city," he murmured to himself. "Immediately."


He checked his pocket watch.


2:38 PM.


Behind schedule by nine minutes.


Arthur slung his rucksack higher on his shoulder, tucked \1 into the heavy hammer loop of his work trousers, and slipped quietly down the sidewalk toward 8th Street.


Three miles to St. Jude Clinic.


Above the distant hospital towers, the purple clouds were beginning to swirl into a violent, funnel-shaped vortex.


Arthur gripped the strap of his bag and broke into a steady, tireless jog.
