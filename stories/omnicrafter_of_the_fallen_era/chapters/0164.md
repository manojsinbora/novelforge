# Chapter 164: The Continental Apex Convergence

The Sawatch Range was the undisputed spine of the continent. Rising in a monolithic, unbroken wall of crystalline Precambrian granite and biotite gneiss along the western margin of the Upper Arkansas Valley, the range contained fifteen of the fifty-four highest peaks in North America. At its geometric center rose Mount Elbert—a colossal, broad-shouldered pyramid of ancient stone ascending to fourteen thousand four hundred and forty feet above mean sea level.

Mount Elbert was not merely a geographic high point; it was the thermodynamic and hydrologic apex of North America. Precipitation falling upon its eastern glaciers drained into the Arkansas River, flowing through the plains to the Mississippi and into the Gulf of Mexico; precipitation falling upon its western slopes plunged into the Roaring Fork, joining the Colorado River on its violent descent toward the Gulf of California. Beneath this crest of stone ran the continental suture line—a deep crustal convergence where planetary telluric currents, circulating through the pre-Cambrian lithosphere, intersected in a dense, multi-phase electromagnetic knot.

For the Sovereign Confederation, whose engineering doctrines treated geology, thermodynamics, and electromagnetism as interdependent systems of a single machine, Mount Elbert was the solitary site on the continent capable of grounding the planetary Harmonic Focusing Array.
