# Chapter 38: The Goliath of Black River

The wind screaming through the Black River Gorge carried the biting, sulfurous reek of subterranean volcanic vents and boiling mountain water.

*Day 6 // 12:18 PM.*

Standing on the edge of the western granite abutment, Arthur Vance adjusted the diopter of his brass terrestrial telescope, his clear rubber safety goggles reflecting the vertiginous four-hundred-foot drop to the roaring white-water rapids below.

The engineering marvel before him was colossal in scale: **The Goliath Cantilever Bridge**.

Constructed in 1932 by the American Bridge Company under the master design of legendary bridge engineer David Steinman, the structure was an iconic monument of heavy riveted structural steel. Spanning six hundred and twenty feet across the abyss, the bridge was engineered to withstand severe mountain seismic tremors and Cooper E-72 heavy steam locomotive train loadings, incorporating twelve thousand tons of high-tensile silicon steel.

The bridge was divided into two massive two-hundred-and-ten-foot anchor arms pinned into the canyon granite, flanking a central two-hundred-foot **Center Swing Span**.

Under normal twentieth-century operations, the two-hundred-foot center truss rotated ninety degrees on a massive fifty-foot-diameter circular granite pivot pier rising from the roaring riverbed. The entire two-thousand-five-hundred-ton dead weight of the swinging span rested on an intricate circular **Roller Nest**—a mechanical track of forty-eight conical forged-steel rollers, each sixteen inches in diameter, driven by a circular rack-and-pinion ring gear forty feet across.

Now, however, the central swing span was frozen open at a dead right angle to the railway line.

A terrifying four-hundred-foot gap of empty air separated the western approach tracks from the eastern shore.

More alarming was the condition of the central pivot pier.

Encrusted around the sixty-foot circular granite base, swallowing the massive cast-steel rack-and-pinion turning gears in an impenetrable layer of bubbling, charred organic slag, was a towering thirty-foot biological redoubt: **The Cinder-Chitin Hive**.

Through his high-magnification optical lens, Arthur analyzed the organisms crawling across the bridge's structural lattice:

They were twelve to fifteen feet long, resembling grotesque, heavily armored fusions of deep-sea king crabs and volcanic burrowing beetles. Their carapaces were six inches of jagged, carbonized basalt and iron slag, constantly radiating visible thermal heat waves. From their thoracic vents, puffs of caustic sulfur dioxide gas hissed into the cold air. Their massive front claws—thick, four-foot shears of hardened bio-metallic chitin—clicked against the steel bridge girders with heavy, ringing acoustic clangs that resonated through the steel trusses.

"Classification: **Cinder-Chitin Titans**," Arthur murmured, his number-two pencil scratching in his field ledger. "Tier Two Specialized Hydrothermal Arthropods. They have colonized the pivot pier due to the localized volcanic sulfur springs venting beneath the riverbed. Their bio-calcareous secretions possess an exceptionally high compressive strength—exceeding ten thousand PSI—and have physically cemented the bridge’s forty-foot turning rack into a solid block of mineralized slag."

Beside Arthur, Captain Rachel Cross lowered her binoculars, her brow knit in tactical calculation.

"If the swing span can't rotate," Cross observed, "there is no rail connection to the eastern continent. We're stranded on this side of the gorge."

Suddenly, from the far eastern abutment—six hundred feet across the roaring chasm—a bright red magnesium distress flare shot into the azure sky.

*FZZZZZZT-POP!*

The brilliant crimson flare arched over the gorge, drifting slowly beneath a miniature parachute in the mountain gale.

"Look at the eastern watchtower!" Sergeant Cole shouted, pointing across the canyon.

On the concrete roof of the eastern bridge operator's cabin, two figures in tattered military camouflage fatigues were waving an olive-drab signal flag. 

Behind them, barricaded behind concertina wire and sandbags, stood a fortified military compound stamped with faded white lettering: **United States Army Logistics Depot — Fort Sterling Outer Perimeter**.

Arthur did not hesitate. 

He unslung a polished brass **Aldis Heliograph Signal Shutter** from his surveyor’s kit, mounting the mirror on the tripod. He caught the midday sun in the concave mirror, aimed the alignment pinhole directly at the eastern cabin, and began operating the thumb-actuated brass shutter lever in crisp, rhythmic Morse code:

*DIT-DIT-DAH-DIT... DAH-DAH-DIT... DIT-DIT-DIT...*

Across the gorge, an answering blink of pale yellow light flickered from the cabin’s observation window:

```
[TRANSMISSION LOG // OPTICAL MORSE CODE]
EASTERN ABUTMENT: "IDENTIFY YOURSELVES. ARE YOU CIVILIAN SURVIVORS?"
WESTERN ABUTMENT: "THIS IS ARTHUR VANCE, CHIEF ARCHIVIST, BLACKWOOD CONFEDERATION. 
ACCOMPANIED BY COMPANY B, 116TH ENGINEER BATTALION, WITH 160-TON DIESEL VANGUARD."
EASTERN ABUTMENT: "GOD BE PRAISED! THIS IS SERGEANT MAJOR HANK BRADLEY,

440-VOLT THREE-PHASE WESTINGHOUSE TURNING MOTOR GEARED TO THE PINIONS.

```

Arthur clicked the heliograph shutter closed, noting Bradley’s transmission in his ledger.

"Fort Sterling is intact," Arthur briefed the team, his voice carrying calm, authoritative gravity. "Nine surviving soldiers holding the eastern terminal under Sergeant Major Bradley. They have maintained continuous radio silence to prevent marauder location-finding, but their visual reports indicate four subterranean concrete warehouses containing seventy thousand gallons of winterized diesel fuel, sixteen high-power HF radio transceiver sets, two million rounds of linked small-arms ammunition, and twenty-five tons of dehydrated military field rations."

Captain Cross’s eyes widened with profound strategic relief. 

"Twenty-five tons of rations and seventy thousand gallons of winterized diesel..." Cross breathed. "That would solve our logistical fuel reserves for the entire Confederation through the spring."

"And more importantly," Arthur pointed out, "the eastern transcontinental rail line beyond Fort Sterling connects directly to the Great Inland Grain Basin and the Pacific coastal maritime ports. If we re-establish this bridge, we transform our regional sanctuary into a continental trade corridor."

"And a hundred-horsepower turning motor waiting in the middle of the bridge," Cross added, her eyes flashing with tactical focus. "Arthur, how do we get three-phase electrical power out to that center pivot pier across four hundred feet of empty air?"

Arthur pointed toward the massive structural steel catenary and telegraph support cables that ran along the top chords of the bridge truss.

"The bridge's original four-hundred-and-forty-volt power line was suspended inside a three-inch galvanized steel conduit running along the lower maintenance catwalk," Arthur explained. "The conduit is physically continuous, though the copper wiring was severed by thermal expansion during Day Zero. We will pull a fresh four-ought copper feeder cable through the conduit using our steel winch wire."

Arthur turned to Eleanor, whose green eyes were fixed on the steaming biological hive on the center pier.

"Eleanor," Arthur said gently. "What does your resonance detect from the core of that hive?"

Eleanor stepped to the edge of the granite cliff. Her Starlight Sovereign frequency pulsed with a deep, resonant emerald-and-gold harmonic that vibrated against the rocks.

"There is an apex heart inside the gear casing, Arthur," Eleanor whispered. "A queen. The **Cinder-Chitin Broodmother**. She is thirty feet long, curled directly around the central kingpin gear. She is channeling the volcanic steam from the riverbed into her eggs. Her shell... it's like liquid iron cooled into basalt. If you hit it with ordinary bullets, you won't even scratch her."

"A high-temperature basaltic matrix," Arthur deduced, nodding slowly. "Basaltic bio-chitin has a melting point of twelve hundred degrees Celsius and an extreme thermal expansion coefficient. However, materials with high thermal expansion possess a fatal thermodynamic vulnerability: **Catastrophic Cryogenic Thermal Shock**."

Arthur turned toward the supply flatcar behind *The Iron Vanguard*.

"Miller! Davis!" Arthur commanded. "Unload four seventy-five-pound cylinders of **Pressurized Liquid Carbon Dioxide** from our firefighting equipment lockers. Bring three heavy copper delivery horns and fifty feet of reinforced cryogenic delivery hose."

Arthur unrolled his canvas tool roll on the flatcar deck, selecting a brass high-flow pressure regulator and three wide-angle spray diffusers.

"Observe the thermodynamic mechanism," Arthur explained to Cole, who was examining the heavy gray cylinders. 

"At ambient cylinder storage pressure of eight hundred and fifty PSI, carbon dioxide is a saturated liquid. When discharged through a calibrated expanding nozzle into atmospheric pressure, the rapid adiabatic expansion and the **Joule-Thomson effect** drop the fluid temperature instantaneously to minus one hundred and nine point three degrees Fahrenheit—solidifying forty percent of the jet into a dense, high-velocity snow of solid dry ice."

He looked at Captain Cross and Sergeant Cole.

"When that cryogenic solid impacts a biological carapace heated to eight hundred degrees by hydrothermal volcanic steam, the localized temperature gradient will exceed nine hundred degrees in zero point two seconds. The resulting instantaneous volumetric contraction will generate tens of thousands of pounds of internal tensile stress, shattering the Broodmother's basalt armor like brittle window glass."

Arthur raised both forearms, the slate-gray star-chitin plates of the **Chimera Frame Mark III** locking into airtight readiness with a sharp pneumatic hiss:

*SNICK-CLACK.*

The twin nitrogen cylinders rested at **Three Thousand PSI**. The three star-iron induction rings on the Sovereign Aegis buckler hummed at seventy-five thousand volts potential.

"The structural transit plan is as follows," Arthur Vance briefed, pointing his pencil toward the massive bridge truss:

"1. The Catwalk Approach: The western anchor arm features a two-foot-wide structural inspection catwalk suspended fifty feet below the rail deck, constructed from galvanized steel grating and reinforced with two-inch wire-rope safety lifelines. We will clip into the safety cables with forged alloy carabiners. The wind shear in the gorge is currently thirty-two knots; maintain three points of physical contact at all times.
2. The Power Tether: Miller and Davis will feed two hundred yards of four-ought copper feeder cable from *The Iron Vanguard's* auxiliary diesel generator along the catwalk handrail, providing three-phase 440-volt power directly to the center machinery house.
3. Decapitation: Captain Cross, Sergeant Cole, and I will breach the central pivot machinery enclosure, shatter the Broodmother's thermal carapace with our cryogenic carbon dioxide diffusers, and execute a kinetic pile-driver strike to clear the pivot gear teeth."

Sergeant Cole checked the chamber of his M4 carbine, his eyes hardened with grim military determination. "And Bradley’s boys on the other side?"

"Once the swing span rotates into alignment, forty-four tons of *The Iron Vanguard* will cross the Black River in forty-five seconds," Arthur replied. "The supplies of Fort Sterling will be secured, and the transcontinental artery will be opened to the eastern sea."

Arthur reached into his waistcoat, verified his brass pocket watch, and clicked it shut with a crisp, authoritative snap.

*1:12 PM.*

"We have two hours before the afternoon wind shear through the gorge exceeds forty knots," Arthur Vance said, adjusting his spectacles.

"Fasten your safety harnesses. Lower your respirators. Inspect all carabiner gate locks and ensure your cryogenic valve keys are securely tethered to your wrists. We are going to reclaim the Goliath."

Sergeant Cole tugged on his nylon harness straps, locked the carabiner onto the high-tensile guide cable, and racked his carbine bolt with grim, steady discipline. Beside him, Captain Cross adjusted her tactical radio throat microphone and tested the flow rate on her liquid carbon dioxide delivery horn.

Arthur stepped onto the first steel rung of the vertical maintenance ladder, his work boots gripping the frost-glazed rungs with unhurried, measured certainty.

Four hundred feet below, the white-water rapids of the Black River churned into roaring, icy foam.

The assault on the center pivot had begun.
