# Chapter 36: The Locomotive Works of Vulcan

The nocturnal shift inside the heavy erecting shop of Vulcan Junction was illuminated not by sputtering torches, but by the clean, glaring white light of two-thousand-watt mercury vapor lamps.

*Day 5 // 8:42 PM.*

Spanning four hundred feet of structural brick and traveling gantry crane rails, the locomotive repair shop was an untouched cathedral of heavy motive power. 

Outside, twenty tons of hard red winter wheat flour from High Rock Commune were being baked into thousands of fresh loaves in the foundry’s restored brick ovens, feeding seventy steelworkers and their exhausted families. Down in the powerhouse, five megawatts of hydroelectric current flowing from Iron Ridge Dam hummed through the shop’s primary three-phase motor banks.

In the center pit of the erecting shop, resting on twelve forty-inch drop-forged steel wheels, sat an industrial titan of the mid-twentieth century:

**The Baldwin-Westinghouse DR-12-8-1500.**

Known in railroading lore as the "Centipede" heavy freight locomotive, the machine was an absolute colossus: eighty feet long, twelve feet high, and weighing an astonishing one hundred and sixty tons. 

Equipped with two eight-cylinder, supercharged De La Vergne diesel engines driving four massive Westinghouse direct-current traction generators, the locomotive had been designed in 1948 to haul five thousand tons of iron ore over the steep mountain divides of the Cascades.

When the rail system fell into obsolescence in the late 1970s, the great machine had been preserved inside Vulcan’s dry storage bay, slathered in cosmoline rust-inhibitor and drained of lubricants.

Arthur Vance stood beneath the front pilot beam, wearing clean denim coveralls over his gray waistcoat, an aluminum clip-board in his left hand and a brass dial-indicator micrometer in his right.

His brass mechanical pocket watch lay open on the steel deck of his rolling tool chest.

*9:14 PM.*

"Bore diameter on Cylinder Bank A: twelve point seven five zero inches," Arthur recorded, his number-two pencil moving with clinical precision. "Maximum cylinder taper: zero point zero zero one five inches. Crankshaft main journal clearance: three thousandths. The mechanical rotating assembly is ninety-six percent free of brinelling and fatigue fissures."

Beside Arthur, Master Foundryman Joseph Kovacs and Old Man Henderson were using steam-degreasing lances to strip forty years of amber cosmoline from the massive chrome-nickel connecting rods.

"She’s a beast, Arthur," Kovacs rumbled, his thick gray beard glistening with condensed steam. "Three thousand horsepower between those two supercharged prime movers. When I was a boy in the fifties, I saw this very engine pull seventy loaded hoppers of taconite ore up a three percent grade without slipping a single driver."

"Her original operational parameters were formidable," Arthur acknowledged, adjusting his wire-rimmed spectacles. "However, standard mid-century copper windings and lead-babbitt sleeve bearings will experience severe thermal degradation if subjected to continuous starlight resonant conduction. We must modernize her internal nervous system before she turns a wheel."

For eight hours through the cold mountain night, Arthur Vance, Joseph Kovacs, Old Man Henderson, and twelve master machinists undertook a complete metallurgical and electrical metamorphosis of the locomotive.

Working with the fresh heats of **Star-Infused 136-Pound Alloy Steel** poured from Blast Furnace Number Four only hours earlier, they rebuilt the machine from the rails up:

**1. The Combustion Liners and Valve Train:**
The original cast-iron cylinder liners were pulled using a fifty-ton hydraulic sleeve puller. In their place, Arthur and Kovacs machined sixteen replacement liners from solid billets of star-infused alloy steel on the shop's thirty-six-inch Lodge & Shipley engine lathe. With a tensile yield strength of eleven hundred megapascals and a diamond-polished bore, the new liners eliminated blow-by and allowed turbocharger boost pressure to be safely increased from eight PSI to a blistering **Twenty-Four PSI**.

**2. The Fuel Injection Calibration:**
On the shop's diesel test bench, Henderson and Arthur disassembled the twin sixteen-cylinder mechanical Bosch jerk-type fuel injection pumps. 

Every fuel injector nozzle was cleaned in an ultrasonic kerosene bath to clear forty-year-old carbon deposits. Arthur mounted each injector on a hand-cranked hydraulic pop-tester, meticulously adjusting the internal spring shims until the crack-pressure needle released at precisely **Three Thousand Five Hundred PSI**, producing a crisp, atomized conical spray with zero nozzle drip. 

"Injection timing dialed to eighteen degrees before top dead center," Arthur recorded. "Combustion droplet distribution: forty microns. Thermal efficiency will exceed forty-one percent."

**3. The Governor and Radiator Overhaul:**
Arthur disassembled the locomotive's primary Woodward PG mechanical-hydraulic governor, replacing its dried rubber diaphragm seals with viton gaskets and filling the hydraulic circuit with low-temperature synthetic oil. The governor was calibrated to maintain idle speed at three hundred and fifty RPM, with a maximum governed redline of eight hundred and fifty RPM.

Simultaneously, Kovacs’s foundry crew flushed the locomotive’s twin five-hundred-gallon cooling radiators with a five-percent caustic soda solution to strip calcified scale, pressure-testing the brass core tubes to forty PSI with clean river water.

**4. The Armored Cab and Defensive Mounts:**
Under Captain Cross’s direction, two combat engineers used heavy cutting torches to fabricate protective armor for the locomotive's operator cab. 

One-inch-thick plates of newly rolled star-infused steel were welded across the forward cab face, angled at forty-five degrees to deflect incoming anti-materiel rounds. Over the forward windows, heavy hinged louvers of three-quarter-inch alloy plate were fitted with narrow, bulletproof laminated vision blocks. 

Above the cab roof, Sergeant Cole welded a reinforced ring mount capable of accepting an M2 Browning fifty-caliber machine gun, giving the locomotive three-hundred-and-sixty-degree defensive fire capability.

**5. The Traction Motor Rewiring:**
The four Westinghouse 370-DA direct-current traction motors mounted in the four-axle articulated trucks were dropped into the pit. Arthur unspooled six hundred pounds of high-purity, oxygen-free copper bus wire, interlacing the armature coils with micro-braided filaments of star-iron harvested from the Sky-Rift Wyrm. The resulting compound windings reduced internal electrical resistance to near absolute zero, allowing the four traction motors to deliver a staggering **Sixty-Five Thousand Pounds of Continuous Tractive Effort** without motor overheating.

**6. The Chimera Induction Shunt (The Vanguard Circuit):**
Above the dual prime movers, Arthur installed a custom-fabricated secondary electrical cabinet: **The Resonance Induction Shunt**. 
Inlaid into the steel locker were three concentric rings of refined star-iron tailings, wired through heavy silver bus bars directly into the locomotive’s main generator field coils. When Eleanor was aboard, her Starlight Sovereign resonance could be injected directly into the locomotive’s electrical grid, transforming the entire 160-ton iron engine into a rolling electromagnetic anchor.

At 4:48 AM, Arthur torqued the final cylinder head stud to seven hundred foot-pounds with a six-foot hydraulic torque multiplier.

He wiped his hands on a clean cotton rag, climbed up the cab ladder, and checked the pressure gauges on the newly installed Westinghouse 26-L air brake console:

*Main Reservoir:* One hundred and forty PSI.  
*Brake Pipe:* Ninety PSI.  
*Tractive Ampere Meters:* Calibrated to twelve hundred amperes.

Arthur opened the throttle quadrant, turned the prime mover start toggle, and pressed the heavy brass starter button.

*CHUG-CHUG-CHUG-CHUG... ROOOOOOOOOOOOAR!*

Both sixteen-cylinder supercharged De La Vergne diesels erupted into life with a deep, earth-shaking baritone rumble that rattled the corrugated iron roof and vibrated the concrete floor of the entire erecting shop.

Twin plumes of clean, gray diesel exhaust puffed from the roof stacks, settling within seconds into a smooth, whistling idle that purred with the terrifying, restrained power of three thousand mechanical horses.

Eleanor stepped into the cab, her wool coat zipped against the predawn chill. When she laid her hand upon the steel reverser handle, the star-iron induction shunt on the electrical locker flared with soft, deep sapphire luminescence.

The locomotive's interior lights, gauges, and windshield defrosters blossomed into clear, warm gold light.

"She feels alive, Artie," Eleanor whispered, her green eyes wide with awe as she listened to the rhythmic, sixteen-cylinder heartbeat beneath the floor. "She isn't just an engine. She’s a moving fortress."

Arthur stepped onto the exterior walkway, where Captain Rachel Cross, Sergeant Marcus Cole, and seventy steelworkers were gathered around the pit.

Kovacs stepped forward, holding a gallon can of high-gloss railway enamel and a stencil brush.

"What do we call her, Arthur?" Kovacs asked, his voice filled with pride.

Arthur looked along the eighty-foot flank of dark, oil-quenched steel, resting his gloved hand on the massive pilot plow.

"She is the locomotive of the Blackwood Confederation," Arthur Vance declared calmly. "The spearhead of our rail network. Paint her: **The Iron Vanguard**."

Kovacs dipped his brush into white enamel and painted the name across the nose in bold, six-inch block letters:

```
THE IRON VANGUARD // UNIT 101
BLACKWOOD CONFEDERATION RAILWAYS
TRACTIVE EFFORT: 65,000 LBS // 3,000 HORSEPOWER
```

Arthur reached into his waistcoat, pulled out his brass pocket watch, clicked it open, and verified the time.

*5:52 AM.*

"Engine break-in cycle complete," Arthur announced, shutting his watch with a decisive snap. "Crankcase oil pressure: sixty-two PSI at idle. Coolant temperature: one hundred and sixty-eight degrees Fahrenheit. Tractive capability: fully nominal."

He looked at Captain Cross, then at Joseph Kovacs, his sharp eyes sweeping across the assembly floor.

"Master Kovacs. Couple the specialized mechanized rail-laying flatcars, the spiking cranes, and sixty tons of our new one-hundred-and-thirty-six-pound star-infused rail behind the tender."

For the next forty minutes, the erecting shop yard was an orchestration of disciplined mechanical assembly. Under Arthur’s precise blueprints, four specialized heavy flatcars were coupled behind *The Iron Vanguard*:

1. **The Continuous Rail Sled (Car 51):** An eighty-foot heavy flatcar equipped with roller guide racks holding forty-eight thirty-nine-foot sections of freshly rolled 136-pound star-infused rail steel—over eighteen hundred linear feet of heavy track per load.
2. **The Hydraulic Tie-Inserter (Car 52):** Fitted with dual hydraulic handling arms salvaged from an industrial timber skidder, capable of grabbing two hundred creosoted oak cross-ties from the cargo deck and seating them onto the graded rock ballast at precise twenty-two-inch spacing.
3. **The Pneumatic Spiking Gantry (Car 53):** A traveling steel gantry frame carrying twin Chicago Pneumatic industrial spike-drivers, plumbed directly into *The Iron Vanguard’s* massive one-hundred-and-forty-PSI main air reservoir, capable of driving four hardened steel track spikes every six seconds.
4. **The Mobile Thermite Crucible Car (Car 54):** Manned by four master foundrymen under Kovacs's veteran foreman, equipped with pre-heating propane torches, graphite rail-welding molds, and fifty canisters of copper-aluminum oxide thermite powder for field-welding rail joints into continuous ribbons of seamless steel.

As the mighty four-car industrial train pulled onto the main departure ladder, fifty steelworkers and their families lined the gravel right-of-way, cheering and waving their leather foundry caps in the crisp morning air.

Master Kovacs walked up to the cab step, extending an eight-inch polished brass commemorative builder’s plate stamped with the original 1948 Baldwin-Westinghouse locomotive serial number.

"Take this with you, Arthur," Kovacs said, his booming voice soft with profound gratitude. "That machine was built to tame mountain ranges and build nations. In your hands, it’s going to do it all over again."

Arthur took the brass builder's plate, bolting it securely to the cab's interior bulkhead beside the reverser quadrant.

"We do not merely lay rail, Master Kovacs," Arthur Vance said, adjusting his wire-rimmed spectacles with quiet, unyielding authority. "We construct the permanent nervous system of human society."

He stepped to the controls, gripped the polished brass throttle lever of *The Iron Vanguard*, and hauled it back to **Notch Two**.

*CHUG-CHUG-WROOOOOOOOOM!*

The twin sixteen-cylinder supercharged diesels roared with thunderous majesty. The Leslie five-chime locomotive horn echoed off the blast furnace towers—a mighty, triumphant baritone blast that rolled across the entire valley.

The one-hundred-and-sixty-ton iron titan lurched smoothly forward, leading the mechanized rail-laying consist east onto the transcontinental mainline.

The march of the iron frontier had begun.
