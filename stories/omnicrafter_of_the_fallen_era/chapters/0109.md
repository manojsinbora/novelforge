# Chapter 109: The Edgar Thomson Converters

The heavy star-iron flanged wheels of the *Prometheus* rumbled along the riverbank sidings of the Monongahela River, following the southern water grade ten miles upstream from Point State Park to the borough of Braddock.

*Day 40 // 2:30:00 PM.*

Arthur Vance sat at the central drafting console of the locomotive’s command salon. Outside, the gray light of mid-afternoon filtered through the persistent industrial haze, reflecting off the oily, ice-scummed surface of the river. 

Arthur’s gold pocket watch lay open upon its green velvet pad. Its jeweled escapement ticked with quiet, absolute precision.

*Snap.*

He closed the hunter case, slid it into his charcoal wool waistcoat, and lifted his mechanical drafting pencil.

"Distance traversed from the Three Rivers confluence: nine point eight statute miles," Arthur recorded in the transit ledger. "Ruling track grade: zero point zero eight percent along the historic riverbed terrace. Track condition: sixty-pound Carnegie Bessemer rail rolled in nineteen hundred and eight, exhibiting twenty-two percent web corrosion and severe gauge widening on curves. Convoy speed restricted to fifteen miles per hour to prevent flange derailment."

Master Kovacs walked into the salon, holding an unrolled linen blueprint clamped between two scarred brass weights. Behind him stood Captain Rachel Cross and Eleanor Vance.

"We’ve crossed under the Turtle Creek viaduct, Arthur," Kovacs said, pointing out the forward quartz windscreen. "Take a look. Carnegie’s original masterworks. The granddaddy of the entire American steel empire."

Arthur rose from his chair, stepped to the observation port, and adjusted his wire-rimmed spectacles.

Sprawled across more than two hundred acres of the river flatlands lay the colossal industrial skeleton of the Edgar Thomson Steel Works.

Founded in 1872 by Andrew Carnegie and named in honor of the president of the Pennsylvania Railroad, the facility had been the first in the nation to produce Bessemer steel rails on an industrial scale. Across two centuries of pre-Collapse expansion, it had grown into a titanic manufacturing complex: soaring blast furnace stoves clad in riveted carbon steel plates, monolithic dust catchers, towering gas washers, twin 250-ton basic oxygen furnace vessels, and a quarter-mile-long continuous slab-casting hall with high structural monitor roofs.

Yet two generations of post-Collapse stagnation had reduced this industrial monument to a state of near-dormant decay.

Only one small, crude foundry cupola was currently operating, fed by scrap and dirty beehive coke brought down by river barge. Thick, yellow-black plumes of toxic sulfur dioxide and unwashed anthracene oil belched from an improvised brick stack, settling like poison over the rusted rail yards. 

A skeleton garrison of sixty Iron Barony metalworkers—clad in grease-stiffened leather aprons, iron-toed boots, and cracked wire safety goggles—watched with stunned disbelief as the gleaming silver-and-charcoal armored train of the Sovereign Western Confederation rolled smoothly into their central marshaling yard.

*PSSSSSS-CLACK.*

The *Prometheus* engaged its regenerative pneumatic brakes and hissed to a complete stop beside the central blast furnace cast house.

"Ambient air metrics at the facility gate," Arthur stated, reading from the command cab's telemetry dials. "Sulfur dioxide: four point six parts per million. Carbon monoxide: thirty-two parts per million. Heavy metal particulate: four hundred and ten micrograms per cubic meter. The atmospheric toxicity is a direct consequence of low-temperature bituminous coke combustion in unsealed hearths."

Arthur turned to Master Kovacs and Captain Cross.

"Our objectives for the afternoon are threefold," Arthur announced, his voice crisp and authoritative. "First: secure and remediate the primary blast furnace hearth, eliminating slag skulls and refractory fracturing. Second: replace the primitive, sulfur-heavy coke combustion cycle with our modular plasma-assisted hydrogen-methane direct reduction tuyere system. Third: restore the basic oxygen furnace vessel and twin-strand continuous slab caster to execute the first heat of high-manganese cryogenic star-steel in the Pittsburgh basin."

Kovacs grinned broadly, cracking his heavy knuckles. "Two hundred acres of dead iron and rusted cranes, Arthur. We’ve got six hours of daylight and pioneer shifts ready to roll. Let's wake the old girl up."

*2:45:15 PM.*

Within fifteen minutes of the train’s arrival, the industrial yard erupted into coordinated, surgical engineering activity.

Three pioneer work-trains detached their heavy crane gantries. Pneumatic star-iron impact wrenches echoed through the vast steel-framed cast house as crews cleared decades of abandoned scrap, fallen corrugated iron roofing, and frozen river mud from the main transfer rail tracks. 

Simultaneously, Captain Cross established an outer perimeter, stationing Sovereign sentries along the Turtle Creek rail bridges to ensure the local scrap scavengers and Barony coal syndicates did not interfere with the high-voltage remediation operations.

Arthur Vance and Master Kovacs entered the towering shadow of Blast Furnace No. 1—a ninety-foot-tall steel stack encircled by three massive refractory-brick hot blast stoves.

Arthur approached the hearth jacket, running the tip of a magnetic ultrasonic thickness gauge across the exterior steel shell.

"Hearth shell thickness: two point two five inches," Arthur noted, writing the figures on a clipboard. "Corrosion allowance remaining: eighty-two percent. However, internal inspection is required."

Arthur peered through an unbolted inspection hatch into the belly of the dormant furnace. 

Inside lay a massive, solid mass of solidified iron and slag—a frozen 'slag skull' weighing an estimated seventy-five metric tons, choked solid when the pre-Collapse operators had been forced to abandon the blow without executing a proper furnace flush.

"A cold freeze," Kovacs observed grimly, shining a high-intensity carbide torch into the black cavern. "If you try to blast that out with black powder, you’ll shatter the magnesite-carbon refractory lining. If you try to burn it out with standard oxy-acetylene torches, it'll take four weeks and twenty thousand cubic feet of gas."

"Conventional chemical cutting is obsolete," Arthur said calmly. "We will utilize the *Prometheus*' auxiliary zero-point plasma manifold."

Arthur turned to the pioneer electrical team:

"Deploy four modular star-iron high-frequency plasma cutting lances. Connect directly to the train’s primary busbar at ten kilovolts. Gas carrier: pressurized argon-hydrogen blend at one hundred and twenty pounds per square inch."

Four technicians clad in aluminized heat-reflective suits stepped forward carrying twelve-foot-long tubular lances forged from cryogenic star-iron. Heavy, insulated copper power cables trailed back to the locomotive's electrical tap-box.

At Arthur’s command, the lances were inserted through the furnace tuyere coolers directly against the frozen iron skull.

*ZRRRRRRR-BOOM!*

A blinding, sapphire-white arc of thermal plasma erupted from the lance tips. 

The plasma jet reached an instantaneous core temperature of sixteen thousand degrees Fahrenheit—three times hotter than the surface of the sun. The seventy-five-ton frozen slag skull did not merely soften; it liquefied into an incandescent river of molten silicate and iron within seconds. 

Liquid slag poured out through the newly cleared iron notch into heavy star-iron refractory ladles positioned below the runners, leaving the furnace hearth clean, smooth, and thermally pre-heated without cracking a single refractory brick.

"Slag skull cleared in fourteen minutes and twenty-two seconds," Arthur recorded, checking his pocket watch.

*3:48:40 PM.*

"Now," Arthur said, stepping toward the furnace bustle pipe—the massive four-foot-diameter insulated steel manifold encircling the furnace waist. "We address the fundamental thermodynamic flaw of twentieth-century ironmaking."

The Baron's chief foundry master, an elderly, soot-streaked man named Tobias Miller who had joined the inspection group, stepped forward hesitantly.

"Flaw, sir?" Miller asked, his voice raspy from decades of breathing coke fumes. "Carnegie and the old-timers blew coke through these tuyeres for a hundred years! It’s the only way to smelt iron from red dirt! You feed iron ore, coke, and limestone into the top, blow twelve-hundred-degree air into the bottom, and the carbon strips the oxygen!"

Arthur Vance turned to Miller, his expression earnest and pedagogical.

"Your historical observation is correct, Master Miller, but your thermodynamic efficiency is abysmal," Arthur explained, tapping his pencil against a freshly drafted process diagram. "A traditional coke-fired blast furnace operates at an overall thermal efficiency of barely thirty-four percent. Sixty-six percent of your energy is lost in sensible heat through the top gas, incomplete combustion of carbon monoxide, and cooling jacket radiation."

Arthur held up a piece of raw bituminous coal from the yard bin:

"More critically, your bituminous coking coal contains between zero point eight and one point four percent sulfur. When that coke combusts in the furnace, that sulfur dissolves directly into the molten iron. In your finished steel plate, sulfur segregates along grain boundaries as iron sulfide (FeS), which has a low melting point of nine hundred and eighty-eight degrees Celsius. When your blacksmiths attempt to roll or forge that plate hot, it suffers from catastrophic hot-shortness—it tears and crumbles along the grain boundaries. That is why your Barony armor plate is brittle, weak, and incapable of withstanding modern kinetic impacts."

"Can't be helped," Miller muttered defensively. "Unless you have low-sulfur charcoal or high-grade anthracite from Scranton, that’s the iron you get."

"It can be entirely eliminated," Arthur countered, "by removing carbon combustion from the primary reduction zone."

Arthur pointed to the twenty-four circular tuyere ports encircling the furnace bosh.

"Master Kovacs, initiate the **Plasma-Assisted Hydrogen-Methane Injection Retrofit**."

For the next ninety minutes, the Sovereign engineering corps executed a masterclass in advanced metallurgical retrofitting.

The heavy, primitive cast-iron blowpipes were unbolted and removed. In their place, Kovacs' team installed twenty-four precision-machined star-iron dual-concentric tuyere nozzles. 

Each nozzle was equipped with an internal zero-point radio-frequency induction heating collar and a central supersonic gas injection lance. 

Instead of blowing ambient air enriched with dirty pulverized coal, the new tuyeres were plumbed into two high-pressure stainless steel gas manifolds routed directly from the train's synthetic chemical synthesis car.

The first manifold supplied purified hydrogen gas ($H_2$); the second supplied reformed methane ($CH_4$).

Arthur climbed the staging ladder to inspect the gas control skid, his micrometer verifying the injector orifice diameters.

"Injection thermodynamics," Arthur explained aloud for the expedition log. "The chemical reduction of hematite iron ore ($Fe_2O_3$) via plasma-assisted hydrogen-methane occurs through direct gaseous reduction:

$$\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta, \text{ Plasma}} 2\text{Fe} + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\Delta H^\circ = +98.8 \text{ kJ/mol})$$

$$\text{CH}_4 + \text{CO}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Induction Catalysis}} 2\text{CO} + 2\text{H}_2 \quad (\Delta H^\circ = +247.3 \text{ kJ/mol})$$

$$\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + 4\text{CO} \rightarrow 3\text{Fe} + 4\text{CO}_2$$

"By superheating the reducing gas mixture to three thousand two hundred Kelvin using localized plasma arcs at each tuyere tip," Arthur stated, "the reduction kinetics are accelerated by a factor of eighteen. The primary reaction byproduct is not toxic sulfur dioxide or dense carbon soot; it is pure, superheated water vapor ($H_2O$) and high-purity carbon monoxide, which is immediately recycled back into the top-gas closed loop.

"Thermal efficiency rises from thirty-four percent to eighty-two point four percent. Coke consumption is reduced by eighty-five percent, serving merely as a permeable structural bed for burden gas permeability rather than a primary fuel source. And sulfur content in the tapped hot metal is suppressed below zero point zero zero three percent—three parts per million."

Tobias Miller stared at the gleaming star-iron injectors with wide, reverence-filled eyes. "Three parts per million sulfur? That’s... that’s laboratory purity on an industrial scale!"

"That is baseline engineering standards," Arthur said flatly, stepping down from the ladder.

*5:15:30 PM.*

With the blast furnace retrofit complete, attention shifted immediately to the downstream refining infrastructure: the Basic Oxygen Furnace (BOF) Shop and the Continuous Slab Caster.

The BOF building was a cathedral of heavy industrial steel. Suspended between massive trunnion rings sat a ninety-ton converter vessel lined with pitch-bonded magnesite refractory. 

Kovacs’ technicians restored electrical power to the vessel’s two-hundred-and-fifty-horsepower tilting drive motors, routing clean zero-point power through new copper busbars. The massive gearsets groaned, turned, and then smoothed into a rhythmic, grease-cushioned purr as the vessel tilted forward to sixty degrees and back to vertical without a shudder.

Above the vessel, the water-cooled oxygen lance was inspected. Arthur replaced the worn copper nozzle with a four-hole supersonic star-iron lance tip designed to deliver pure cryogenic oxygen at Mach 2.2, along with pulverized high-calcium lime flux for instantaneous slag formation and dephosphorization.

Two hundred yards to the east, Eleanor Vance stood within the high bay of the continuous twin-strand slab caster.

The caster was a technological marvel of the late twentieth century: a curved-track machine designed to take molten steel directly from a transfer ladle, pour it through a refractory tundish into oscillating copper molds, spray-cool it with thousands of high-pressure water jets, and bend it continuously into an endless solid ribbon of glowing slab steel.

Eleanor walked slowly down the casting platform. Her fingers lightly brushed the cold copper mold plates. 

Around her fingertips, a soft, warm radiance of violet starlight pulsed in gentle synchronization with the machine’s internal geometry. The ancient First Dynasty resonance seemed to awaken the dormant spirit of the steelworks, harmonizing the mechanical linkages and aligning the atomic structures of the rollers.

"The cooling channels are free of scale, Arthur," Eleanor called out, her voice clear and melodious over the hum of the work-train generators. "The hydraulic oscillators are calibrated to one hundred and twenty cycles per minute. The steel is ready to flow."

Arthur approached the casting console, nodding respectfully.

"Thank you, Eleanor. We will now charge the blast furnace."

*6:00:00 PM.*

The skip hoist engaged. 

Tons of refined Lake Superior taconite pellets—transported from the Great Lakes fleet via the Cleveland rail corridor—were hoisted up the steep skip bridge and dumped into the top bell of Blast Furnace No. 1. Along with the ore went high-purity limestone flux and a precise ten-percent charge of metallurgical coke to maintain column permeability.

Arthur Vance stepped to the blast control desk.

He drew his pocket watch, checked the time, and clicked the case shut.

*6:12:00 PM.*

"Initiate hydrogen-methane gas feed," Arthur ordered through the command radio. "Engage plasma ignition at all twenty-four tuyeres. Set cold blast volume to forty thousand cubic feet per minute at forty-two pounds per square inch."

*VHOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOSH!*

A deep, sub-audible roar shook the concrete foundation of the cast house.

Through the armored quartz sight glasses of the tuyere blowpipes, the combustion was not the dirty, flickering orange of burning coal. It was a terrifying, beautiful vortex of blinding, electric sapphire and gold. 

The plasma arcs ionized the incoming hydrogen and methane, creating a localized thermodynamic inferno of three thousand Kelvin. 

Inside the furnace stack, the red taconite pellets dissolved into liquid metal almost instantly. The high-purity hydrogen gas ripped oxygen atoms from the iron molecules with ferocious chemical efficiency, generating roaring plumes of clean steam that whistled through the top-gas scrubbers.

Within forty-five minutes—a fraction of the eight to twelve hours required by a traditional coke blast—the furnace hearth filled with molten hot metal.

*7:15:20 PM.*

"Hot metal temperature: sixteen hundred and forty degrees Celsius," Master Kovacs bellowed, reading an optical pyrometer. "Silicon: zero point four percent. Carbon: four point one percent. Sulfur: zero point zero zero two percent! Arthur, she’s ready to tap!"

"Tap the furnace," Arthur said.

A pioneer technician triggered a pneumatic star-iron rock drill mounted on the cast floor. 

*RAT-TAT-TAT-TAT-CLANG!*

The clay plug in the iron notch shattered.

A blinding, liquid torrent of molten iron erupted from the furnace. It roared down the refractory-lined runner in a glowing, golden-orange cascade, showering the cast house in millions of glittering iron sparks. 

The metal poured directly into a hundred-ton refractory transfer ladle mounted on a rail car below. 

The air grew intensely, scorchingly hot, radiating dry waves of thermal energy that smelled not of soot, but of pure, clean molten element.

The railcar transported the full ladle into the adjacent BOF shop. 

The 250-ton overhead charging crane lifted the ladle smoothly, tilting it over the open mouth of the Basic Oxygen Furnace vessel. Molten iron cascaded into the vessel with a thunderous *crackle-boom*. 

Immediately, Arthur directed the addition of the proprietary Sovereign alloying elements: high-purity electrolytic manganese ore from the Shenandoah stockpiles, refined aluminum ingots, vanadium carbide, and a calibrated dose of pulverized star-iron flux.

The oxygen lance lowered into the vessel neck.

*ROOOOOOOAR!*

Pure supersonic oxygen blasted into the molten bath at Mach 2.2. A magnificent volcanic plume of incandescent white flame erupted from the vessel mouth into the water-cooled exhaust hood. 

The oxygen burned out the excess carbon, dropping it from 4.1% to a precise 0.40%, while the star-iron flux and manganese dissolved into the liquid matrix, rearranging the crystal lattice into an ultra-tough, austenitic microstructure.

For precisely eighteen minutes, the blow continued.

Then the lance retracted. The vessel tilted smoothly on its trunnions.

*7:52:10 PM.*

"Tap the converter into the casting ladle," Arthur commanded.

The liquid steel—now transformed into high-manganese cryogenic star-steel—poured into the casting ladle at a blinding white-gold temperature of twenty-nine hundred and eighty degrees Fahrenheit.

The crane hoisted the ladle to the top turret of the continuous slab caster.

The ladle slide-gate opened. 

Molten star-steel flowed smoothly into the tundish, and from the tundish down into the oscillating copper mold.

High-pressure water sprays hissed violently as thousands of gallons of clean water struck the descending steel shell, solidifying a uniform outer crust of dense, austenitic crystal. The heavy star-iron guide rolls gripped the glowing ingot, drawing it downward in a sweeping ninety-degree arc from vertical to horizontal.

At eight fifteen in the evening, the first continuous slab emerged onto the runout table.

It was a colossal ribbon of flawless, orange-hot steel—ten inches thick, sixty inches wide, moving at a steady seventy inches per minute. 

An automated traveling oxy-plasma torch clamped onto the moving slab, slicing cleanly through the ten-inch steel with a shower of brilliant azure sparks, cutting a perfect twenty-foot slab weighing nearly twenty metric tons.

Arthur Vance walked along the elevated catwalk beside the runout table. He wore his heavy leather welding gauntlets and dark safety goggles. 

He rested a portable ultrasonic hardness tester and a magnetic eddy-current probe against the glowing end of the cut slab.

The digital readout chimed cleanly.

"Yield strength: nine hundred and sixty megapascals," Arthur read the data aloud, his voice entirely steady. "Ultimate tensile strength: thirteen hundred and eighty megapascals. Elongation to failure: forty-six percent. Charpy V-notch impact energy at minus one hundred and ninety-six degrees Celsius: one hundred and eighty-four Joules. Brittle-to-ductile transition temperature: nonexistent. The steel remains fully ductile at liquid nitrogen temperatures."

Master Kovacs let out a joyous, thunderous roar that echoed through the quarter-mile casting hall, clapping his soot-caked hands together. 

"Cryogenic star-steel!" Kovacs shouted. "In the heart of Braddock! With this plate, we can build locomotive hulls that can withstand point-blank howitzer impacts! We can build pressure vessels for deep-earth geothermal taps and star-iron cog rails that will never wear out!"

Behind them, Baron Gregory Sterling and a dozen of his senior commanders and ironmasters walked slowly onto the casting platform. 

Sterling stared at the endless glowing ribbon of slab steel, then looked up at the blast furnace stacks outside. 

There was no black, suffocating soot falling from the sky. The air inside the mill was clean, warm, and breathable. The river water flowing through the discharge flumes was clear and cool.

Sterling turned toward Arthur Vance, his rough face etched with an emotion that was almost religious in its intensity.

"You took our dead monster... and made it breathe clean air," Sterling said quietly, his voice catching in his throat. "In three hundred years of ironmaking, this valley has never seen metal like that."

Arthur removed his dark safety goggles, revealing his calm, analytical eyes. He reached into his coat and produced a heavy brass-bound folio containing the formal industrial constitution of the Sovereign Western Confederation.

"Engineering is not magic, Baron Sterling," Arthur replied calmly. "It is the rigorous application of fundamental natural laws to material matter. What was missing from this valley was not raw labor or courage; it was precise thermodynamics and ethical stewardship."

Arthur laid the open vellum folio upon a clean steel drafting desk beside the caster control station.

He picked up his mechanical drafting pencil, checked the graphite lead, and wrote with crisp, indelible strokes:

Arthur Vance handed the brass fountain pen to Baron Gregory Sterling.

"Affix your signature to the Charter, Baron Sterling," Arthur said. "As of this moment, the Edgar Thomson Works is reborn as the **Keystone Metallurgical Foundry**. It will operate under the dual jurisdiction of the Pittsburgh Municipal Council and the Sovereign Western Confederation Corps of Engineers."

Baron Sterling took the pen without hesitation. With a bold, firm hand, he signed his name across the parchment, sealing the pact with the heavy stamp of his signet ring.

Arthur took the pen back, signed the document as Chief Archivist, and added the gold seal of the Confederation.

He withdrew his gold pocket watch from his waistcoat.

He pressed the release catch.

*Click.*

The hands stood precisely at the half-hour.

*8:30:00 PM.*

The hunter case clicked shut.

Arthur Vance looked out across the vast, illuminated casting bay, where the glowing star-iron slabs continued their measured, unstoppable march down the runout tables into the waiting storage yards.

"The Keystone Metallurgical Foundry is officially commissioned," Arthur Vance announced. "Pioneer shifts will rotate to maintenance schedules. Tomorrow at dawn, we begin rolling structural plates for the Allegheny Mainline."
