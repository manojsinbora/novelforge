# Chapter 11: The Thermite Foundry

The temperature in Workshop Section 3 climbed past eighty-five degrees as the twin oxyacetylene torches hissed against the steel welding table.

Arthur Vance adjusted his shaded number-five cutting goggles, tapped the end of a magnesium ribbon into an aluminum powder crucible, and stirred the gray mixture with a ceramic rod.

*2:14 PM.*

Beside him, Miller was grinding the rusted steel casing of an industrial speaker magnet on the heavy pedestal wheel. Bright orange sparks rained down into a bucket of water, hissing into white clouds of steam.

"Stoichiometric ratio," Arthur observed without looking up, his voice cutting through the shriek of the grinder with practiced clarity. "Three parts pulverized ferric oxide to one part pure aluminum powder. When ignited by a magnesium electrical bridge, the reaction achieves four thousand five hundred degrees Fahrenheit in zero point four seconds."

Miller dunked the cooled magnet into the water and wiped it on his denim apron. "That's hot enough to melt a locomotive axle into soup, Vance."

"That is precisely the design objective," Arthur replied.

On the wooden bench behind them sat twelve completed magnetic demolition canisters. 

Arthur had machined the housings from three-inch hydraulic cylinder tubing. On the bottom of each cylinder sat two powerful neodymium speaker magnets salvaged from the hospital’s triage intercoms, capable of eighty pounds of magnetic pull against cast iron. Along the top sat a spring-loaded percussion striker wired to a two-inch magnesium ribbon fuse and an electrical primer cap.

Clamped to the track idler of a forty-nine-ton armored bulldozer, the magnetic canister would direct a focused jet of molten white-hot iron straight through the track tensioner, welding the drive sprocket and track chain into a solid, immovable puddle of slag.

"Twelve charges complete," Arthur said, clicking the safety cotter pin into the final canister. "Sufficient to immobilize all three of Marcus Cole's scrap-dozers, with six reserves for rail switches and fuel infrastructure."

Davis walked into the workshop carrying an armful of olive-drab canvas tool rolls, his twelve-gauge shotgun slung across his back. "The truck is ready, Arthur. We stripped the machine gun mount off the bed, baffled the tailpipe through an old water muffler, and checked the tires. From fifty yards, she sounds like a purring kitten."

The vehicle was the second armored technical captured from Vance Rogan's squad that morning. 

Arthur had deliberately left the jagged white snarling-wolf emblem painted on the doors, along with the grease stains and dented scrap armor. In the dim, violet-tinged twilight of the basin below, the truck was an invisible passport into syndicate territory.

"Davis, Miller," Arthur said, stripping off his leather welding gloves and hanging them on the wall rack. "We depart in twenty minutes. Pack the siphoning hoses, three ten-gallon fuel bladders, and the sugar-emulsion canisters."

***

At three-fifty in the afternoon, Arthur stood in the bunker's central airlock, strapping the Chimera Frame firmly over his right forearm.

The airlock was cold and quiet. 

Beside the inner blast door, Dr. Ward was checking a crate of emergency pressure bandages, while Eleanor sat on an overturned wooden crate, her green eyes following every adjustment Arthur made to the leather cinches.

She had combed her auburn hair into a neat braid, and the faint sapphire traceries along her collarbone pulsed with a calm, rhythmic warmth beneath her wool sweater.

"You're going into the rail yard," Eleanor said softly. It wasn't a question; it was a simple, quiet statement of fact.

"The Western Siding holds twelve thousand gallons of diesel," Arthur replied, checking the eighteen-hundred-PSI needle on his forearm pneumatic gauge. "If Marcus Cole fuels his heavy drill-dozers, he will reach the library sub-basement before noon tomorrow. Denying him fuel buys seventy-two hours of strategic operational margin."

Eleanor reached into the pocket of her sweater and pulled out a small, intricately woven circle of thin, bright copper wire.

Intertwined within the copper strands was a delicate, glowing thread of blue light—a hair-thin filament of pure starlight energy she had unconsciously channeled into the metal while resting in the medical bay. The circle pulsed with a faint, soothing warmth that smelled faintly of summer rain.

"Take it," Eleanor said, holding it out to him.

Arthur looked at the small metal circle through his spectacles.

"Copper wire of thirty-gauge thickness," Arthur observed neutrally. "Tensile strength is approximately four pounds. As a defensive kinetic ward, its ballistic efficacy is statistically indistinguishable from zero."

"It's not armor, you idiot," Eleanor said, a fond, exasperated smile crinkling the corners of her eyes. "It's a grounding charm. Keep it in your vest. Whenever you get too buried in your blueprints and forget that you're human... it’ll remind you who is waiting for you in this rock."

Arthur paused.

He looked down at his sister’s face—clear, alive, and breathing because of forty-six minutes of desperate, calculated science—and a rare, quiet softness touched his gray eyes.

He took the small copper charm, wrapped his fingers around it, and slipped it neatly into the left pocket of his gray wool waistcoat, right beside his brass pocket watch.

"The conductivity of copper is beyond dispute," Arthur said smoothly. "I shall retain it for empirical circuit evaluation."

Eleanor let out a soft snort of laughter. "Go. And don't dent your shiny arm."

Arthur nodded, turned toward the outer airlock, and pulled his brass-rimmed respirator up over his nose and mouth.

"Miller. Davis. Mount up."

***

The captured technical rolled out of the granite quarry turnaround at exactly four-twenty, descending the steep mountain switchbacks toward the valley basin.

Up on Blackwood Ridge, the air was clear. But six hundred feet below, the Oakhaven basin had vanished beneath a vast, simmering sea of dark purple miasma. 

The sun was sinking behind the western peaks, painting the smog in bruised shades of violet, blood-orange, and soot-black. Far across the valley, the ruined towers of the commercial district jutted from the fog like broken headstones in an overgrown cemetery.

Arthur sat in the passenger seat, his left hand resting on the dashboard, his right arm resting easily in the padded sleeve of the Chimera Frame.

"Two miles to the industrial perimeter," Davis whispered from the driver’s seat, his boots feathering the accelerator to keep the baffled exhaust to a low, muffled hum. "Road is clear of wreckage. Looks like Cole’s patrols have been keeping this spur plowed for their supply runs."

"Stay on the gravel shoulder," Arthur instructed through his respirator. "Avoid the asphalt crown. Wet rubber against cold tar generates acoustic signatures that travel four hundred yards in dense air."

Fifteen minutes later, the iron fencing of the **Central Rail Yard Locomotive Depot** loomed out of the violet gloom.

It was a fortress of industrial iron and brutal scrap engineering.

Surrounding the forty-acre rail switchyard stood a twelve-foot perimeter fence of heavy chain-link, topped with triple coils of razor wire and electrified by thick copper cables running from a roaring diesel generator shack. Every fifty yards, elevated watchtowers constructed from oil-derrick scaffolding swept the perimeter with halogen searchlights.

In the center of the yard sat the **Locomotive Roundhouse**—a colossal, semi-circular brick behemoth spanning three hundred yards, its smoke stacks belching black coal smoke into the purple sky.

Outside the roundhouse, parked in a defensive echelon beneath floodlights, sat Marcus Cole’s mechanical horrors:

Three **Caterpillar D9 Bulldozers**.

They were terrifying monstrosities. Their yellow cabs had been completely encased in two-inch welded scrap armor plate, bristling with gun ports and barbed wire. Across the front of each forty-nine-ton machine hung a massive triangular plow blade, and mounted over the engine hoods were heavy, engine-driven hydraulic auger drills—industrial drilling rigs scavenged from the municipal subway extension, designed to bore ten-foot shafts through solid granite.

"Look at those drills," Miller breathed from the back seat, his jaw clenched tight. "Those things could chew through the library basement like cardboard."

"And without diesel fuel," Arthur said calmly, "they are merely sixty tons of stationary scrap metal."

Arthur pointed through the windshield toward **Siding 4** on the western perimeter.

Sitting on an unlit industrial siding, guarded by two elevated light towers, sat a string of six black cylindrical rail tanker cars. Stenciled in faded white paint along their curved steel flanks were the words:

```
UNION TANK CAR CO. // HAZMAT 1993
HIGH-SULFUR DIESEL FUEL // 20,000 GALLONS
```

"Cole’s fuel reserve," Davis muttered. "Two guards on the catwalk above the valves. One roving sentry with an assault rifle near the switch stand."

"Park the truck thirty yards behind the grain elevator," Arthur commanded softly. "Kill the headlights. Miller, prepare the fuel bladders and the transfer siphon. Davis, stay with the vehicle and cover the egress lane with the shotgun."

"And you?" Davis asked.

Arthur checked his pocket watch.

*6:12 PM.*

Dusk had settled over the rail yard. The cold mountain wind was whipping purple fog across the tracks, dropping visibility to twenty yards.

"I have municipal maintenance to perform," Arthur said.

He slipped out of the passenger door without making a sound, his rubber-soled boots touching the damp gravel like falling leaves.

Arthur moved through the shadows of the rail yard with the silent, fluid grace of a man who had spent five years navigating the dark, cluttered aisles of a library cellar.

He slid beneath the rusted trucks of an abandoned boxcar, stepped over a rail switch frog, and closed the distance to Siding 4 in forty-five seconds.

The roving sentry—a burly man in a greasy canvas hunting jacket carrying an AK-47—was leaning against the wheel carriage of the third tanker car, smoking a rolled cigarette.

Arthur closed the final ten paces from the sentry's blind angle behind the tanker's heavy brake cylinders.

He didn't draw a weapon.

Arthur reached out with his left hand, clamped a thick cotton cloth over the man’s mouth and nose, and applied two fingers of the Chimera Frame to the carotid sinus on the right side of the sentry’s neck.

A five-thousand-volt micro-pulse surged for two tenths of a second.

The sentry went limp instantly, his cigarette dropping into Arthur’s gloved palm before it could hit the ballast. Arthur snuffed the ember against the heel of his boot, lowered the unconscious man gently beneath the tanker frame, and propped his rifle against the wheel.

"One sentry neutralized," Arthur noted silently. "Zero acoustic leakage."

He climbed the steel access ladder to the primary manifold valve on the second tanker car.

From his toolbag, Arthur pulled out three plastic bottles filled with a viscous, amber-brown liquid: a concentrated solution of supersaturated sucrose syrup, water, and pulverized ferric oxide rust.

Sugar in diesel fuel was a common mechanic’s myth—it didn't dissolve in petroleum. But when combined with a water-alcohol emulsifier and fine iron oxide dust, the suspension stayed homogenously blended until it hit the high-temperature combustion chambers of a heavy diesel engine. At four hundred degrees Fahrenheit, the sugar caramelized into rock-hard carbon lacquer, cementing the fuel injector needles into their barrels and seizing the cylinder rings in fifteen minutes of engine operation.

Arthur opened the tanker’s top inspection hatch and poured the three bottles directly into the fuel stream.

"Twelve thousand gallons of high-grade diesel," Arthur murmured, reseating the brass cap. "Neutralized for heavy mechanized combat."

He stepped off the ladder, preparing to signal Miller for the siphoning run, when a sound caught his ear.

It wasn't the rumble of a patrol truck.

It was a faint, muffled sob.

Arthur froze.

He listened intently, filtering out the rhythmic chug of the distant roundhouse generators and the hum of the electric fence.

The sound was coming from **Car 42**—a weathered, fifty-foot refrigerated boxcar sitting uncoupled at the far end of Siding 4. The heavy steel sliding doors were barred from the outside with a heavy piece of railroad tie jammed through the locking hasps.

Through the narrow four-inch ventilation louvers near the top of the boxcar, the faint murmur of human voices drifted into the cold night air:

"—please, water... the children haven't drank since yesterday—"  
"—quiet, Henderson! The guards will shoot through the slats again—"

Arthur’s boots stopped dead in the gravel.

*Henderson.*

Old Man Henderson. The cantankerous, seventy-two-year-old retired head librarian who had stolen Arthur’s rubber shop goggles three days before the apocalypse.

Arthur stepped up to the ventilation louvers, raised his face to the steel mesh, and spoke in a clear, low whisper:

"Henderson."

Inside the pitch-black boxcar, the murmuring instantly died.

A trembling, elderly voice answered from the dark: "...Who’s there? Is that... is that the guard?"

"Henderson, it is Arthur Vance," Arthur said, his voice flat and deadpan. "You still owe me forty dollars and a pair of vulcanized safety goggles."

A collective, gasping sob echoed from inside the boxcar.

"Arthur?!" Henderson croaked, his voice cracking with wild, disbelieving relief. "My God! Vance! You're alive! They rounded up thirty of us from the library emergency shelter! Archivists, teachers, hospital staff! They’re keeping us in here until the drill begins!"

Another voice—sharp, cultured, and shaking with terror—pressed close to the wooden slats:

"Arthur! It's **Alistair Finch**! The chief curator!"

Arthur’s heart gave a single, hard thump beneath his waistcoat.

Professor Finch was the senior archivist who held the master combination keys to the deep subterranean vault beneath the library—the hermetic archive where the ancient First Dynasty codices were locked.

"Finch," Arthur said, his hands already gripping the heavy wooden railroad tie barring the door. "Status. Are there wounded?"

"Four with fever," Finch whispered hurriedly. "Two children. Cole’s men are using us as human shields for the drill convoy! If you leave us here, we won't survive the night!"

Arthur checked his brass pocket watch under the pale moonlight.

*6:28 PM.*

The simple fuel sabotage mission had just expanded exponentially.

Arthur looked back toward the grain elevator where Miller and Davis were waiting with the technical truck. Then he looked at the massive refrigerated boxcar containing thirty innocent colleagues and the key to the First Dynasty vault.

Arthur Vance adjusted his wire-rimmed spectacles, rested the palm of the Chimera Frame against the heavy oak bar, and engaged the eighteen-hundred-PSI pneumatic switch.

"Henderson," Arthur said through his respirator. "Step back from the doors. The library is officially executing an emergency inter-library loan."
