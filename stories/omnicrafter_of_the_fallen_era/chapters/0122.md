`M42 — AUTHORIZED CENTRAL ELECTRICAL PERSONNEL ONLY`

"The historic M42 rotary substation," Arthur said, running his gloved fingers over the heavy mechanical tumblers of the deadbolt. He unclipped a multi-jawed mechanical bypass tool from his frame harness, inserting the hardened star-iron prongs into the brass cylinder. With three precise, calculated torquing motions, the internal pins aligned.

*Clack.*

The four-hundred-pound steel blast door swung inward on greased bronze hinges.

A wave of air washed over them—not the stale, frozen stench of surface ruins, but the dry, distinctive scent of electrical insulation, transformer varnish, aged paper, and desiccant silica gel.

*2:15:20 PM.*

The stairwell cut straight down through solid rock, descending eighty vertical feet in a steep, iron-grated spiral.

When their boots struck the bottom landing, Arthur raised his induction lantern.

The beam cut through a colossal subterranean vault. Carved directly out of the dark, mica-flecked granite of the Manhattan schist, the M42 rotary converter room was an industrial cathedral of the First Dynasty. Spanning over two hundred feet in length and forty feet in height, the floor was lined with ten massive General Electric synchronous rotary converters.

Each machine was a gargantuan beast of cast iron and laminated silicon steel, fifteen feet in diameter and weighing over forty tons. Their massive cylindrical armatures, wound with heavy copper strap conductors, rested in split-sleeve babbitt bearings between towering stator field poles. Along their collector rings and commutators, heavy brush-rigging assemblies held hundreds of pure carbon brushes in spring-loaded bronze holders.

Running along the western wall of the vault, elevated five feet above the floor on structural steel framing, stretched the high-voltage distribution gallery. Polished black Vermont slate switchboard panels, six feet tall and three inches thick, held open-air knife switches with solid copper blades four inches wide. Arthur knelt by the base of Rotary Converter Number Four. He pulled a four-terminal micro-ohmmeter from his field kit, connecting the calibrated Kelvin clips to the commutator segments.

Miriam Hayes led Arthur, Eleanor, and Captain Cross through a reinforced vault door at the northern terminus of the M42 hall, entering the Inner Repository of the Manhattan Archivist Guild.

The room was vast, silent, and impeccably maintained.

Ranks of floor-to-ceiling steel flat-file cabinets stretched down long aisles, illuminated by small, glowing vacuum-tube filament lamps powered by a bank of sealed glass-jar Edison nickel-iron storage batteries. For two hundred and fifty years, this space had been an abyss of absolute, freezing darkness.

Spanning two hundred and seventy-five feet in length, one hundred and twenty feet in width, and soaring one hundred and twenty-five feet to its vaulted plaster ceiling, the concourse was the grandest interior civic room ever constructed on the North American continent.

Above them, arched sixty-foot clerestory windows let in the cold, fading indigo twilight of the winter evening. The floor was paved in smooth, dusty Botticino marble. In the exact center of the vast expanse, standing atop the brass information booth, rested the famous four-faced clock, its brass casings tarnished with gray scale, its opalescent glass dials dark and frozen at 10:14.
