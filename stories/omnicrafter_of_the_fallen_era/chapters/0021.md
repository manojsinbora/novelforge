# Chapter 21: The Grain Convoy

The smell of freshly ground winter wheat and roasted chicory filled the cold morning air at High Rock Commune.

*Day 3 // 6:15 AM.*

A dense autumn frost coated the wooden rail ties of the timber siding, glittering like crushed quartz in the pale rose light of dawn. 

Along the loading platform of the central granary, fifty farmers formed a bucket-brigade line, transferring sixty-pound burlap sacks of hard red winter wheat and seed potatoes into the cavernous wooden cargo bay of Car 42. 

Beside the boxcar door, Arthur Vance stood with an aluminum clipboard and a yellow number-two pencil, checking each sack as it crossed the threshold.

"Sack count: six hundred and forty," Arthur recorded, his pencil moving with crisp, mechanical strokes. "Total grain payload: nineteen point two metric tons. Moisture content: eleven point four percent. Germination potential: optimal."

Mayor Silas Vance walked up the ramp, carrying two heavy tin canisters of salted sweet-cream butter and a crock of mountain clover honey.

The old farmer looked at Arthur’s clean, meticulous ledger, then at the dual star-iron bracers strapped over Arthur’s gray wool sleeves, and shook his head with an affectionate chuckle.

"Arthur, you're the only man I've ever seen who can drop a thirty-foot subterranean monster at dusk and balance a crop manifest before breakfast."

"The maintenance of accurate agricultural inventory is the foundational prerequisite of civilization," Arthur replied without looking up from his board. "A failure to record seed reserves by weight invariably leads to spring famine. History is replete with empires that fell to improper ledgering."

Silas handed the crock of honey to Eleanor, who was sitting on the locomotive running board, swinging her boots in the crisp air. 

Eleanor took the honey with a radiant grin, her green eyes sparkling. "Thank you, Uncle Silas. Artie hasn't fed me anything but salted beef and emergency biscuits for forty-eight hours."

"Emergency biscuits possess ninety-four percent caloric bio-availability," Arthur pointed out smoothly. "Clover honey is forty percent unrefined fructose."

"It tastes like summer, Artie," Eleanor countered, dipping a wooden spoon into the golden syrup. "Which means it has infinite bio-availability."

Arthur paused, his pencil hovering over the paper. Behind his wire-rimmed spectacles, his left eyelid gave a slow, microscopic twitch.

"An unquantifiable parameter," he murmured, before noting the honey under auxiliary provisions.

From the front of the train, Dr. Nicholas Ward walked back along the gravel ballast, holding a six-inch glass sample tube filled with frothing water taken from the Blackwood River irrigation intake.

The water was ice-cold, but it was not clear mountain runoff. 

Suspended within the liquid were billions of microscopic, glittering flakes of dark metallic dust that swirled in slow, self-organizing geometric currents. When Ward held a small pocket magnet near the glass, the flakes instantly aligned into sharp, needle-like hexagonal lattices.

"Arthur," Dr. Ward said, his voice dropping into quiet, serious focus. "Take a look at this assay."

Arthur took the tube, held it up against the morning sun, and pulled his pocket magnifying loupe from his waistcoat.

"Particulate concentration: two point four grams per liter," Arthur analyzed, his sharp gray eyes tracking the crystalline alignment. "Specific gravity: seven point eight. Non-magnetic ferritic silicates combined with meteoric star-iron."

He dipped a slip of blue litmus paper into the water. The paper turned a rich, deep purple.

"Alkaline pH of eight point two. Ambient water temperature is thirty-eight degrees Fahrenheit, yet the dissolved oxygen levels exceed saturation by forty percent. This is not chemical industrial pollution."

"It's pylon runoff," Eleanor said quietly, stepping down from the locomotive.

She reached out and lightly tapped the glass tube with her index finger.

*PING.*

The moment her starlight-imbued skin made contact with the glass, the metallic flakes inside the water flared with a gentle, pulsing sapphire luminescence—swirling in rhythmic, concentric rings that mirrored the heartbeat in her wrists.

"The Second Star-Iron Pylon is discharging into the reservoir," Eleanor said, looking north toward the sheer granite walls of the river gorge. "The pressure behind the dam is building. The water is trying to conduct the charge, but the spillway gates are choked."

"Distance to the Iron Ridge Hydro-Dam: fourteen point eight miles," Arthur stated, stowing the sample tube in his leather field kit. 

"The logging line terminates at the old turbine construction siding half a mile below the powerhouse. Beyond that point, the canyon walls are vertical granite. We must approach on foot through the lower tailrace tunnels."

Miller threw the heavy locking lever on Car 42's sliding doors, securing the nineteen tons of grain behind padlocked steel bars.

"Train consist is coupled and brake-tested, Arthur," Miller reported, wiping grease from his hands. "We have sixty gallons of diesel in the shunter's tanks and fifty gallons of fresh water in the boiler tender. We're ready to roll."

Arthur turned to Silas Vance, extending his right hand.

The old farmer gripped Arthur’s hand firmly, his calloused palm meeting the cool, smooth star-iron plates of the Chimera Frame.

"Be careful up in that canyon, son," Silas warned, his weathered face shadowed with concern. "The 116th National Guard battalion marched up that gorge on night zero with armored personnel carriers and heavy artillery. None of them ever came back down. If someone is transmitting from that powerhouse... they’ve been trapped in the dark for three days."

"Then we shall provide them with illumination," Arthur said.

He turned toward the locomotive, vaulted up the steel rungs of the pilot ladder, and stepped into the warm, diesel-scented cab behind the controls.

Arthur Vance clicked open his brass pocket watch.

*6:44 AM.*

He pulled down his clear rubber safety goggles, knocked the reverser into forward, and hauled the brass throttle to **Notch 4**.

*CHUG-CHUG-WROOOOOOM.*

Depot Shunter Number 7 whistled once—a long, confident, echoing blast that rolled across the fertile fields of High Rock—and the iron train surged north into the shadow of the mountain gorge.
