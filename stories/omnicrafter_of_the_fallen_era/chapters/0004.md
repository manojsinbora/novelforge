# Chapter 4: The Starlight Ward

The interior of St. Jude Clinic smelled of autoclaved linen, stale sweat, and ozone so concentrated it tasted like sucking on a copper penny.

Arthur followed Dr. Nicholas Ward through the heavy double fire-doors of the ambulance receiving dock. The ground-floor triage ward was bathed in the sickly crimson glare of emergency exit signs. Overhead fluorescent tubes flickered and hummed, their ballasts buzzing like angry wasps as the hospital’s basement diesel generators struggled against an unstable electrical grid.

Along the tiled hallway, thirty or forty civilians were huddled on woolen blankets. Nurses in torn surgical scrubs moved between them with saline bags and pressure bandages. Some victims had eyes bandaged against caustic mist exposure; others lay shivering in cold shock, their skin marked with angry red welts where the purple fog had touched bare flesh.

Nobody spoke above a whisper. The silence was punctuated only by the distant, rhythmic thrumming vibrating through the concrete floor—a slow, forty-beat-per-minute pulse that made the water in the mop buckets ripple in concentric rings.

"Fourth floor, North Wing," Dr. Ward said, leading Arthur past a barricade of overturned steel filing cabinets toward the central stairwell. "Elevators are dead. Central cables sheared twenty minutes into the first tremor."

Arthur glanced through the wired-glass window of the elevator landing as they hurried past. The elevator doors had been forced a foot apart with a pry-bar, revealing an empty shaft that vanished into pitch darkness. But instead of the usual draft of cool cellar air, a shimmering thermal updraft was rising from the pit, carrying glowing purple motes of crystalline dust that drifted upward like embers from a furnace.

"The basement morgue and maintenance bays collapsed entirely," Ward muttered, his hand resting on the banister as they began taking the concrete stairs two at a time. "The fissure tore straight through the municipal bedrock. The bottom of that shaft opens directly into whatever subterranean cavity opened beneath the city."

"Bedrock depth in this district is roughly seventy feet," Arthur replied, his voice muffled behind the brass respirator. His boots struck the concrete treads with mechanical rhythm: two steps per second, perfectly paced to conserve stamina. "Limestone karst over shale. A cavern collapse wouldn't generate thermal updrafts of this velocity without an active geothermal or high-energy mutagenic pressure gradient underneath."

Ward threw a sharp look over his shoulder at Arthur. "You talk like a university geologist, Vance. Most men in your shoes would be hyperventilating."

"Hyperventilation decreases arterial carbon dioxide and induces cerebral vasoconstriction," Arthur said flatly. "Fainting on a staircase will not improve Eleanor’s survival probability."

Ward let out a dry, breathy chuckle that sounded more like a cough. "Fair enough."

They climbed past the second floor. On the third-floor landing—Pediatrics—the air grew noticeably warmer. Here, the hospital walls were no longer clean white plaster. Along the baseboards, delicate, branching tendrils of dark violet crystal were creeping across the vinyl flooring like frost on a winter windowpane. Where the crystals touched the copper water pipes running to the overhead fire sprinklers, the metal had corroded into a brittle, iridescent blue crust.

"Exothermic crystalline bio-growth," Arthur noted, his eyes narrowing behind his spectacles. He reached out with his left hand, tapped the tip of his drop-forged pipe wrench against a three-inch cluster, and watched the crystalline lattice vibrate. "It’s drawing ambient heat and trace minerals from the building’s plumbing. Do not touch it with bare skin. The crystal structure is needle-sharp and likely carries concentrated mutagenic spores."

"We figured that out twenty minutes ago," Ward said grimly. "One of my junior residents brushed against a patch in the ICU. His forearm broke out in black keratin scales within ten minutes. We had to isolate him in the utility closet."

At the fourth-floor fire landing, the air was sweltering.

The temperature had jumped past ninety-five degrees. The scent of ozone was so thick it made the hairs on Arthur's forearms stand on end, even through the heavy cotton sleeves of his smock. Small, harmless-looking static sparks danced across the steel handrails, snapping against Arthur's brass watch chain with tiny, bright blue flashes.

At the end of the corridor stood the heavy, blast-rated steel fire doors of **Medical Ward 4: Intensive Isolation**.

The doors were sealed shut. Around the edges of the frame, the industrial rubber weatherstripping had melted into black, tarry goo. The heavy digital keypad beside the frame was a puddle of melted gray plastic, its internal circuit board scorched into carbon by an intense electrical surge.

"This is as far as anyone got," Ward said, stopping four feet from the door. He unholstered his service revolver, holding it loosely by his hip, though the gesture was clearly born of helpless nerves rather than tactical utility. "The magnetic interlocks fused when the power surged. Even if we had the master keycard, the internal bolt pins are welded into the strike plates."

Arthur stepped up to the door. He laid the gloved back of his hand against the cold-rolled steel plate.

The metal was vibrating. Not with the heavy, low-frequency rumble of the subterranean tremors, but with a high-pitched, singing hum—like the top string of a cello bowed under impossible tension.

*Forty beats per minute.*

Through the small, wire-reinforced observation port in the upper panel of the door, the interior of Ward 4 was visible.

It looked like the aftermath of an air raid.

Eight of the twelve hospital beds had been smashed to matchwood against the far wall. The linoleum floor had torn completely open down the center of the room, leaving a jagged, two-foot-wide abyss that glowed with an eerie, pulsing violet light from the subterranean fissure below. Concrete dust hung in the stagnant, superheated air like a golden fog.

And suspended in the center of the ward, surrounded by a swirling halo of azure luminescence, was **Bed 12**.

The heavy iron bedframe had been anchored to the concrete floor by four heavy floor bolts. Three of those bolts had sheared clean off, leaving the bed tilted at a sickening twenty-degree angle over the edge of the chasm.

Lying on the mattress, strapped into clean white cotton linens, was fifteen-year-old Eleanor Vance.

Her eyes were closed, her face pale as freshly fallen snow. But beneath her translucent skin, intricate patterns of brilliant cobalt-blue light were pulsing along her vascular system—tracing up her wrists, curling around her throat, and illuminating the delicate bone structure of her cheekbones like constellations of cold starlight. Every time her heart beat, a shockwave of azure static rippled off her fingertips, snapping against the iron bedrails with the sound of a whip cracking in the dark.

"Her resonance is accelerating," Arthur said, his voice dropping into absolute stillness. "The subterranean fissure is acting as an external tuning fork. Her bio-electrical field is trying to harmonize with the planetary vein beneath the foundation."

"Can we save her?" Ward asked, his voice shaking slightly. "Vance, if that floor gives way, she drops sixty feet into molten miasma."

"Step back, Doctor," Arthur said.

He slung his canvas bag to the floor and pulled out the gear he had scavenged from the ambulance: the ten percent surgical hydrogen peroxide, the roll of fiberglass splint tape, and the brass-tipped oxygen cylinder, along with his fourteen-inch drop-forged pipe wrench.

"You're going to breach a blast-rated steel door with a wrench?" Ward asked, incredulous.

"Only fools fight tensile strength with blunt force," Arthur replied without looking up. "Materials science is about exploiting thermal differentials."

He took the bottle of surgical peroxide and poured half of it directly into the recessed gap between the double doors where the three-inch steel bolt was seated. The concentrated oxidizer fizzed angrily against the grease and scorched plastic, clearing out the debris.

Next, Arthur retrieved the portable oxygen cylinder. He unthreaded the pressure regulator entirely, exposing the bare quarter-inch brass outlet nozzle.

He inverted the cylinder, jammed the nozzle directly into the seam where the bolt met the strike plate, and struck the emergency relief pin with the flat cheek of his wrench.

*FSSSSSSSSSSSH!*

A concentrated, blinding jet of liquid oxygen—minus two hundred and ninety-seven degrees Fahrenheit—flooded the steel locking bolt.

Within three seconds, the gray steel turned a chalky, frost-covered white. The metal shrieked as microscopic stress fractures raced across its crystalline lattice under the violent, instantaneous contraction.

Arthur dropped the empty cylinder, gripped *The Electric Spanner* in both hands, set the hardened chisel edge against the frosted seam, and slammed his full body weight downward with the leverage of the fourteen-inch handle.

*BANG.*

The frozen steel bolt shattered like an icicle.

The left door swung inward on shrieking hinges, blasted open by the wave of superheated, ozone-laden air from inside the ward.

Arthur didn't hesitate. He pulled his brass respirator up firmly against his bridge, grabbed his rucksack, and stepped across the threshold into Ward 4.

The moment his boots touched the ward floor, the hair all over his body stood erect. A tingling, needle-sharp charge washed over his skin, and the air smelled intensely of scorched lavender and electric lightning.

"Eleanor," Arthur called out, his voice cutting through the hum.

The girl on the bed did not wake, but the blue constellations beneath her skin flared brighter. A sudden arc of azure lightning leaped from the iron bedframe, striking the concrete floor three inches from Arthur’s left boot. The linoleum instantly vaporized, leaving a smoking black scorch mark that smelled of burnt carbon.

Dr. Ward stopped dead in the doorway, raising an arm to shield his face from the blinding blue glare. "Vance! The bio-electric potential is too high! You can't touch her—it will stop your heart!"

"The human body conducts electricity through electrolytes in interstitial fluid," Arthur said, moving forward with steady, measured steps, keeping his eyes fixed on the remaining floor bolt holding the bed above the chasm. "A direct current arc requires a path of least resistance to ground."

He dropped to one knee beside his bag.

He pulled out the roll of fiberglass tape, the jar of silver sulfadiazine burn cream, and a twelve-foot length of heavy copper grounding cable he had torn from the ambulance's high-voltage inverter.

"Silver is the most electrically conductive elemental metal known to science," Arthur said, unscrewing the lid of the burn cream. "One hundred and six percent the conductivity of standard copper. When suspended in a water-soluble emulsion, it creates an instantaneous bio-compatible surface conductor."

He scooped two thick handfuls of the silver cream into his palms. Moving with the delicate precision of a master surgeon binding an ancient parchment, Arthur stepped up to the edge of the chasm.

Below his boots, thirty feet down through the jagged crack in the floorboards, a subterranean lake of molten violet miasma churned and bubbled like boiling oil. The heat was blistering, scorching the leather of his boots.

Arthur reached out and laid his silver-coated hands flat against Eleanor’s bare forearms.

*CRACKLE!*

A violent surge of azure lightning slammed straight into Arthur’s arms.

His vision flashed white. Every muscle in his shoulders locked into a brutal, agonizing spasm. His teeth clicked together so hard his jaw ached, and his heart skipped a terrifying, stuttering beat as fifteen thousand volts of pure celestial static surged through his smock.

"Vance!" Ward yelled in horror from the doorway.

"Hold... the line," Arthur grated through his clenched teeth, his voice a ragged whisper behind the brass mask.

He didn't pull away. He leaned into the agony, using his fingers to spread the thick silver cream in a continuous, unbroken path from her wrists down along her inner elbows, across her shoulders, and straight into the heavy copper grounding cable wrapped around his own waist.

The moment the circuit closed, the dynamics of the room changed completely.

The blue lightning ceased leaping wildly into the air. Instead, the chaotic arcs followed the silver path, flowing smoothly down her arms, into the copper cable, and through the heavy steel clamp Arthur had attached to the reinforced vertical I-beam supporting the hospital’s outer wall.

*BZZZZZZZZZT.*

The structural steel beam of the clinic hummed with a deep, resonant tone as thousands of amperes of raw starlight energy were safely dissipated into fifty tons of architectural iron and deep foundation earth.

The blinding glare in the room softened to a pale, gentle sapphire glow.

The fever lines along Eleanor's neck began to dim. Her shallow, panting breaths slowed, deepening into a steady, rhythmic cadence. The sweltering heat radiating from her skin dissipated, replaced by the cool, fragile warmth of natural sleep.

Her eyelids fluttered.

Behind dark, long lashes, her pale green eyes opened slowly, blinking against the dust and smoke.

She looked at the cracked ceiling. She looked at the smoldering scorch marks on the linoleum. Then her gaze drifted to the right, landing on the strange, soot-covered figure in the gray archivist's smock and brass respirator kneeling at her bedside.

A faint, exhausted smile touched the corners of her lips.

"Arthur..." she whispered, her voice barely louder than a rustle of dry paper. "You brought... the ugly mask."

Arthur reached up with a soot-stained sleeve, carefully adjusted his wire-rimmed spectacles, and clicked open his pocket watch.

*3:18 PM.*

"The mask is functional," Arthur replied, his voice perfectly even, though a subtle, rare warmth softened the corners of his eyes. "And you have four minutes before I record a demerit in your hospital chart for unscheduled pyrotechnics."

Eleanor let out a weak, airy giggle, closing her eyes as the exhaustion of the three-year fever finally gave way to natural, restorative rest.

Dr. Ward stepped cautiously into the room, stepping over the scorched floorboards, his stethoscope already in his hand. He knelt beside the bed, pressed the diaphragm against Eleanor’s chest, and checked her wrist pulse against his pocket watch.

"Pulse seventy-two," Ward murmured, his face a portrait of utter disbelief. "Respirations sixteen. Temperature... ninety-nine point four. It's stable. Vance, in thirty years of medicine, I have never seen a runaway systemic fever drop eight degrees in forty seconds."

"Thermodynamics and basic electrical grounding, Doctor," Arthur said, carefully wrapping a clean sterile bandage over the silver-coated skin of her wrists. "Nothing more."

Before Ward could reply, the hospital foundation shuddered.

It wasn't a tremor. It was a violent, upward heave that lifted the floor three inches into the air.

*CRACK-BOOM.*

Across the room, the far concrete wall split from ceiling to floor. A cloud of mortar dust erupted into the corridor, and from the deep subterranean abyss beneath the elevator shaft, a sound echoed upward that made every window in the hospital vibrate.

It was not the sound of shifting stone.

It was a deep, guttural, earth-shaking **bellow**—a primordial roar that sounded like grinding tectonic plates mixed with the shriek of a thousand hunting reptiles.

The purple clouds outside the fourth-floor windows suddenly whipped into a frenzied vortex. Down in the courtyard, three blocks away, car alarms began shrieking simultaneously as waves of subterranean beasts answered the call from the deep.

Dr. Ward scrambled to the window, his face turning the color of chalk. "The alpha... whatever was sleeping beneath the fault line, Vance... her awakening drew it right to the surface."

Arthur stood up smoothly, slinging his canvas bag over his shoulder. He picked up his drop-forged pipe wrench, checked the capacitor charge on *The Electric Spanner*, and looked down at his sleeping sister.

"We cannot defend a hospital that is falling into a hole," Arthur said calmly.

He leaned down, slid his arms beneath Eleanor's shoulders and knees, and lifted her from the bed with practiced ease. Her head rested against the heavy canvas of his shoulder, her breathing calm and steady under the silver-bandaged wraps.

"Where are we going?" Ward asked, his voice tight as he racked the slide of his service revolver. "The whole city is drowning in mist."

Arthur stepped toward the door, his boots crunching over shattered glass and cold-rolled steel.

"Blackwood Ridge," Arthur said, his eyes hard behind his spectacles. "Bunker Zero-Seven. Three miles past the municipal tree line. It was built during the Cold War into solid granite with independent deep-well water and hermetic blast seals."

He looked back at the doctor.

"Grab your surgical instruments and whatever penicillin is left in the pharmacy, Ward," Arthur said, stepping out into the crumbling corridor. "We have a convoy to organize."
