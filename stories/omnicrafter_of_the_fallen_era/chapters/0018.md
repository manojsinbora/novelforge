# Chapter 18: The Northern Transmission

The twin Caterpillar D17000 diesels of Depot Shunter Number 7 rumbled with a deep, subterranean baritone that shook the pine cones from the branches above the quarry terminal.

*3:38 PM.*

The expedition consist stood ready on the northern spur:
At the point: the forty-four-ton switcher locomotive, its front pilot beam fitted with the triangular steel plow and two high-intensity aircraft landing lights scavenged from the airport supply depot.
Behind the locomotive: Car 42, its wooden interior reinforced with three-quarter-inch steel plating and outfitted as a mobile surgical triage unit by Dr. Ward and Vera.
At the rear: the heavy industrial flatbed car carrying the armored technical truck, chained securely to the deck with high-tensile binder ratchets.

Arthur Vance walked the length of the train, his eyes methodically cataloging the inspection points:

*Air brake pressure:* ninety PSI continuous. Zero leakage at the glad-hand couplings.  
*Sander boxes:* topped off with five hundred pounds of crushed silica grit to ensure mountain grade traction.  
*Auxiliary cargo:* three fifty-five-gallon drums of industrial calcium hydroxide powder—slaked lime—strapped securely to the flatbed deck.

"Calcium hydroxide," Arthur noted to Miller, who was tightening the final ratchet strap with a heavy cheater pipe. "A strong alkaline base with a pH of twelve point four. When brought into contact with the concentrated hydrochloric gastric secretions of subterranean chimeras, it triggers an instantaneous neutralization reaction, generating calcium chloride, water, and sixty-four kilocalories of boiling exothermic heat. It turns biological acid into sterile, harmless salt."

Miller wiped axle grease from his forehead with the sleeve of his jacket, grinning broadly. "In other words, you brought fifty gallons of monster heartburn."

"I brought elementary stoichiometric chemistry," Arthur corrected, without a trace of humor in his voice. "The monsters merely supply the acid."

Dr. Ward and Eleanor climbed onto the locomotive footboard.

Eleanor was wearing her dark field coat, her hands tucked inside leather gloves. Her green eyes were sharp, scanning the dark pine ridges to the north where the jagged peaks of the Blackwood Mountains met a bruised, gray autumn sky.

"Eleanor," Arthur said as he vaulted onto the cab deck. "Sensory report."

Eleanor laid her gloved hand against the cold cast-steel cab frame.

The faint sapphire traceries along her collarbone pulsed once, sending a soft, rhythmic wave through the metal into the rails.

"The radio transmission from High Rock went dead eight minutes ago," Eleanor said quietly. "The burrowers have breached the southern greenhouse perimeter. I can feel the vibrations traveling down the river canyon... thousands of rhythmic, digging heartbeats tunneling through the shale."

"Distance to High Rock Commune: fifteen point four miles," Arthur said, stepping up to the engineer's control stand on the right side of the cab. "Average track speed along the timber spur: twenty-eight miles per hour. We arrive in thirty-three minutes."

He knocked the brass reverser lever into forward, kicked open the air sander valves, and notched the throttle to **Position 4**.

*CHUG-CHUG-WROOOOOOM.*

The locomotive groaned, its steel drive wheels bit into the sand, and the iron train surged out of the Blackwood Ridge quarry, turning north onto the **Old Northern Timber Line**.

***

The timber spur was an engineering marvel from the 1920s, carved by logging barons into the sheer granite cliffs of the Blackwood River Gorge.

On the left, the vertical rock face rose three hundred feet into the sky, crowned with dark stands of lodgepole pine and Douglas fir. On the right, the ground dropped eighty feet into a roaring, frothing canyon where the Blackwood River churned over jagged boulders.

Down in the gorge, the water was no longer clear mountain melt.

It ran in thick, boiling currents of violet and deep iridescent turquoise, carrying foam that hissed against the riverbanks. The mutagenic runoff from the northern fissures was already poisoning the river, eroding the granite bedrock with unnatural chemical speed.

Arthur kept his hand relaxed on the brass throttle stand, his eyes sweeping the tracks ahead through the locomotive's clean glass windshield.

Beside him, Davis was scanning the treeline with a pair of military field binoculars, his twelve-gauge shotgun resting across his knees.

"Timber Mile Six," Davis called out, checking the weathered wooden mileposts flashing past. "Track ballast looks solid. No fallen timber."

"Watch the expansion joints," Arthur replied, his voice calm over the rhythmic drone of the diesels. "Cold river air combined with geothermal subsurface venting creates rapid thermal cycling in sixty-pound rail steel. Look for sun kinks or bowed rail heads."

At Timber Mile Nine, Eleanor suddenly stiffened.

She pulled off her right glove and pressed her bare fingertips against the steel floorboards of the cab.

The sapphire constellations on her skin flared with a sharp, warning flash.

"ARTHUR! STOP THE TRAIN!" Eleanor cried out.

Arthur didn't ask questions. 

He didn't hesitate for a fraction of a second.

His left hand slammed the throttle to idle; his right hand seized the automatic train air brake handle and threw it straight into **Emergency Application**.

*PSHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHH!*

Seventy pounds of train-line air vented into the atmosphere in an instantaneous, deafening roar.

Underneath the locomotive and the trailing cars, every brake shoe slammed against the drive wheels with twenty tons of pneumatic clamping force. 

Sparks erupted from the thirty-three-inch steel wheels in blinding orange showers. The entire forty-four-ton locomotive shrieked in mechanical protest, sliding across the sanded rails before coming to an absolute, shuddering halt fifteen yards from the entrance to **Devil's Gulch Bridge**.

Davis nearly flew into the windshield, catching himself on the handrail with a breathless gasp: "What is it?! What’s on the bridge?!"

Arthur didn't answer. He stepped out onto the front footboard, pulled down his rubber safety goggles, and pointed his inspection lantern at the bridge entrance.

Devil's Gulch was a sixty-foot wooden timber trestle spanning a deep, rocky chasm between two granite ridges.

The rails across the bridge looked normal at first glance. 

However, beneath the central span, fifty feet above the canyon floor, the heavy twelve-inch creosote-soaked timber bents were actively disintegrating.

Swarming across the central bridge pilings were dozens of creatures the size of large badgers, covered in thick, segmented plates of bone-white chitin. They possessed no eyes; instead, their blunt, armored snouts ended in four rotating, calcified mandibles that operated like industrial rock drills, grinding into the seasoned timber and stone piers with a high-pitched, wet buzzing sound:

*bzzzz-crunch, bzzzz-crunch.*

From their mouths oozed a thick, smoking green bile that sizzled against the wood, dissolving thirty years of structural creosote into steaming, blackened pulp.

"Grave-Chitin Burrowers," Arthur analyzed, his voice dead level as he stepped down from the pilot ladder into the ballast. "Early Tier Two subterranean detritivores. High mandibular torque; acidic digestive secretions consisting of sixty percent concentrated formic and sulfuric acid. If the locomotive had hit that center span at twenty miles per hour, the bents would have buckled under the dynamic load, dropping forty-four tons into the gorge."

"They're eating the bridge!" Davis yelled, scrambling down the ladder with his shotgun raised. "Arthur, how the hell do we cross?!"

"With alkaline stoichiometry," Arthur said.

He sprinted back to the flatbed car, unclipped two five-gallon steel buckets of the dry calcium hydroxide powder, and grabbed his heavy pipe wrench.

"Miller! Davis! Grab the buckets!" Arthur ordered. "Do not shoot them! Lead buckshot will merely shatter their chitin plates, spraying pressurized acid into the wooden stringers!"

Arthur stepped onto the railroad ties of Devil's Gulch Bridge.

He moved with astonishing speed across the ties, his boots landing with light, sure-footed balance over the sixty-foot drop. 

Below him, the burrowers sensed the vibrations in the rails. Three of the blind beasts stopped gnawing on the bridge piers, raised their dripping, mandibular snouts toward Arthur, and hissed—a sharp, spraying sound like punctured radiator hoses.

They arched their armored backs, preparing to regurgitate a pressurized stream of green acid directly at his boots.

Arthur didn't give them time to vomit.

He inverted the steel bucket, dumping twenty pounds of dry, pure white calcium hydroxide powder directly down into the central timber bent.

*FOOOOOOM.*

The fine white alkaline dust enveloped the swarm in a suffocating cloud.

The reaction between the concentrated biological acids and the strong calcium hydroxide was instantaneous, violent, and utterly unforgiving.

*KZZZZZZZZZZT!*

The acid in their open mouths boiled instantly into white-hot steam. 

The exothermic neutralization reaction reached three hundred degrees in two seconds flat. The burrowers shrieked—a high-pitched, chittering wail of agony—as the boiling calcium salts sealed their respiratory spiracles and neutralized their digestive mandibles into harmless, chalky crust.

The creatures lost their grip on the timber bents, tumbling fifty feet down into the rocky riverbed below like falling stones.

Within thirty seconds, the central bridge bent was silent.

Arthur lowered his empty bucket, pulled his inspection lantern from his belt, and examined the wooden bridge stringers.

"Structural loss: twenty-eight percent," Arthur calculated, tapping the charred timber with the heel of his pipe wrench. "The core Douglas fir heartwood is uncompromised, but the lateral cross-bracing is severed. We cannot support the locomotive's static weight without tensile reinforcement."

"How do we reinforce a railroad bridge in the middle of nowhere?!" Miller asked, staring over the edge at the drop.

"We use the technical truck's recovery winch," Arthur said.

Working with furious, mechanical coordination, Arthur, Miller, and Davis ran the truck’s twelve-thousand-pound hydraulic steel winch cable across the top chord of the bridge timber. 

Arthur looped the cable through the bridge's main structural kingpost, hooked the forged steel eye back onto the truck’s tow hitch, and engaged the hydraulic tensioner until the steel cable sang with three tons of upward pre-tension.

By converting the damaged wooden trestle into an underslung cable-stayed suspension truss, Arthur redistributed eighty percent of the locomotive’s downward weight into the solid granite bedrock anchors on the south rim.

*4:52 PM.*

Arthur tightened the final cable clamp, wiped a drop of sweat from his forehead, and jogged back to the locomotive cab.

"Miller, set the technical’s emergency brake and lock the winch drum," Arthur commanded. "Davis, Eleanor—mount up. We cross Devil's Gulch at four miles per hour."

Arthur notched the throttle into Position 1.

The 44-ton switcher locomotive rolled onto the wooden bridge with a slow, agonizingly steady crawl: *creak... groan... clack.*

Beneath the wheels, the heavy steel winch cable strained, singing with taut, resonant tension as it carried the massive weight across the abyss. 

The locomotive crossed the center span. The wood held. The cable held.

With a final metallic clatter, the wheels rolled off the timber ties onto the solid granite roadbed of the north rim.

Behind them, Car 42 and the flatbed truck glided safely across the chasm.

Eleanor let out a long, trembling breath, her hand relaxing against the window frame. "We made it..."

Arthur didn't celebrate.

He pointed through the front windshield toward the crest of the northern mountain ridge two miles ahead.

Rising above the dark pines was a towering pillar of thick, oily black smoke. 

Through the trees, the frantic crackle of automatic rifle fire and the muffled thud of exploding propane tanks echoed across the valley, accompanied by high-pitched, predatory shrieks that sounded like a flock of hunting raptors descending upon a kill.

Arthur Vance reached into his waistcoat, clicked open his brass pocket watch, and noted the time.

*5:14 PM.*

He slammed the brass throttle forward to **Notch 8**, and the iron train surged into the valley at full battle speed.
