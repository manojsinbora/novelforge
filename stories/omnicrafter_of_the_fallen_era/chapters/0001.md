# Chapter 1: The Cracking Stacks

The Dewey Decimal System had never accounted for the end of the world, which Arthur Vance found personally offensive.

He was standing on the fourth rung of an oak rolling ladder in the Sub-Basement Archive, three floors beneath the Grand Municipal Library, attempting to shelve a worn, leather-bound volume titled *Subterranean Pressures and Mine Ventilation*. It belonged in Section 540, right between an old text on furnace metallurgy and a grease-stained field manual for steam boilers.

Arthur adjusted his wire-rimmed spectacles with the back of his wrist. He was twenty-three years old, lean and quiet, wearing an archivist’s gray smock that smelled permanently of cedarwood, book glue, and dried mint. To the chief librarian, Arthur was merely the assistant who took thirty minutes too long to sweep the floors.

In reality, Arthur took thirty minutes too long because he refused to shelve any book he had not completely read. In five years among the municipal stacks, he had read all forty-two thousand volumes in the repository. He knew how much shear force an oak beam could bear before snapping; he knew how to mix basic workshop compounds from memory; and he knew, with chilling certainty, that the shudder running through the floorboards was not a burst water pipe.

*Crack.*

The sound was deep, dull, and felt straight through the heels of his boots.

A second tremor hit, harder than the first. Mortar dust sifted from the brick vaults overhead, drifting like gray snow across the mahogany display cases. Across the aisle, an entire row of city survey records jumped off the shelves and slammed against the floor.

Arthur didn’t shout. Panic burned through oxygen and wasted energy. Instead, he stepped down from the rolling ladder, clicked his pocket stopwatch, and pressed his palm against the flagstones.

The stone was heating up. Fast.

According to the city survey maps, the bedrock beneath the library was solid granite, dormant for millions of years. Yet beneath his fingers, the stone pulsed with a slow, heavy thrum—like a giant heart beating beneath the city.

Then the floor tore open.

With a deafening shriek of splitting slate, a jagged fissure ripped across the room from the maintenance door straight through Section 14.

Out of the trench poured a heavy, rolling fog. It wasn't chimney smoke or furnace soot. It was thick, shimmering with a dark purple sheen, rolling across the floorboards like cold liquid. The moment it hit the cellar air, the sweet, sickly scent of rotting jasmine and sharp ozone filled the room.

*The ancient mist,* Arthur thought, his eyes narrowing behind his glasses.

Months ago, while cleaning a crate of forgotten manuscripts, he had found a torn parchment fragment salvaged from an excavation outside the city. It spoke of deep planetary cracks, ancient slumbering power, and an unearthly mist that drove beasts and men mad. At the time, he had filed it under folklore.

Now, that folklore was pooling around his ankles.

Upstairs, the screaming started.

It began as a muffled scramble on the ground floor—patrons shouting, chairs overturning, a stampede of feet rushing toward the street exits. But within seconds, the voices changed. The screams turned wet, harsh, and guttural, broken by the sickening sound of snapping bone and tearing wood.

Arthur pulled a small, brass-cased pocket radio from his smock. He flicked the dial through static until a manic voice cut through the speaker:

"—emergency bulletin from Civil Defense! Subterranean ruptures reported across all downtown districts! Citizens are ordered to stay indoors and avoid the purple vapor! High biological hazard—repeat, do not inhale the haze—"

The broadcast distorted violently. A dispatcher's panicked yell bled into the background:

"—containment failure at St. Jude Clinic! West wing is breached! Medical Ward 4 is cut off—the fever patients are reacting to the air! Their vitals are—"

A blast of white noise swallowed the transmission, leaving only a dead hum.

Arthur’s fingers clenched around the radio until the brass casing pinched his palm.

*Ward 4. St. Jude Clinic.*

Four miles west. Straight through the busiest part of the city.

His fifteen-year-old sister, Eleanor, had spent the last three years in Isolation Ward 4. The doctors called her condition an unknown degenerative illness. Her body ran feverish day and night, and whenever the attacks peaked, pale blue lines flickered beneath her pale skin like glowing thread. 

None of their medicines had worked. Two days ago, the head physician had warned Arthur that Eleanor’s body was wearing down.

*It was never a disease,* Arthur realized, his pulse quickening. *Her blood was reacting to whatever is down there.*

If the purple fog filled that ward, her fever would spike out of control. He had hours, maybe a couple of days at most, before she couldn't hold on any longer.

"Right," Arthur said quietly into the empty room. "Step one: don't breathe the fog. Step two: get to Eleanor. Step three: kill whatever gets in the way."

The purple haze was already rising past his knees.

Arthur turned toward the restoration bench. He didn't waste a second. On the table sat his daily book-cleaning tools: a glass flask of alcohol, jars of activated charcoal powder used to absorb moisture from damp pages, rolls of clean linen tape, and an old brass workshop mask with a screw-on filter canister.

He worked with brisk, steady hands.

He dumped two generous scoops of crushed charcoal into the canister—porous carbon was the best natural trap for airborne toxins. He soaked a thick cotton filter pad in a diluted vinegar wash to neutralize alkaline particulates, pressed it firmly against the charcoal, and screwed the brass mesh cap down tight. 

He pulled the mask over his face and cinched the leather straps behind his ears.

He took a breath.

The air pulled through the canister was cool, dry, and clean. No jasmine. No ozone sting.

*Good,* he thought. *Simple, but it works.*

He slipped the ancient manuscript fragment into an oiled leather pocket inside his smock, along with his pocket watch.

Just as he reached for his coat, the heavy oak door leading to the upper stairs gave a violent shudder.

*BANG.*

The iron bolt snapped. The door flew off its upper hinge, groaning in protest, and a heavy shape squeezed through the broken frame.

It had been the library’s daytime security guard only ten minutes ago. Now, his navy uniform hung in bloody tatters over an unnaturally swollen frame. His right arm had thickened into a massive limb clad in rough, charcoal-black scales, tipped with four-inch claws. His jaw was distended, crowded with jagged teeth, and his eyes were completely clouded over with milky white film.

The creature lowered its heavy head, nostrils twitching, snorting wetly as it tested the cellar air. 

A Tier 1 Novice mutant, caught in the violent first stage of corruption.

Arthur stood behind the long oak workbench, completely still.

*Sight is gone,* Arthur observed silently, his mind clicking into gear. *It's hunting by sound and scent. Skin on the shoulders looks tough as tree bark, but the throat is pale, soft, and exposed. No armor there.*

The beast snarled, dragging its heavy claws along the brick wall, taking a slow step toward the smell of sweat and leather.

Arthur glanced down at the floor beside him.

Sitting against the wall was a half-used fifty-pound canvas sack of quicklime—crushed masonry powder used by the cellar repair crew to dry out damp walls. Beside it, on the low shelf, sat two heavy glass jugs of industrial muriatic acid used to strip mineral scale from the pipes.

He had no sword. He had no magical powers. But he knew what happened when concentrated acid met dry lime.

Arthur nudged the canvas sack with the toe of his boot, tipping it over. A thick mound of dry white powder spilled across the damp stones directly in front of the counter.

The rustle of canvas drew the beast immediately. It let out a wet roar and lunged forward, claws raised.

Arthur hoisted the glass jug of acid, stepped back two paces, and hurled it straight down into the white mound.

*CRACK.*

The glass shattered. The acid hit the lime.

The reaction was instantaneous. A blinding, violently hot cloud of caustic white steam erupted into the air, expanding like an explosion.

The creature charged straight into the plume. 

It took a single ragged breath of the boiling vapor. The caustic mist seared its throat and burned through its lungs. The beast choked, its roar cutting off into a strangled wheeze. It clawed wildly at its own neck, thrashing against the oak bench until its airway clamped completely shut.

*THUD.*

The creature slammed face-first into the flagstones, twitched for five seconds, and lay still.

Arthur blew out a breath through his filter. "Quicklime and acid. Cheap, ugly, and effective."

Before the smoke could clear, the rear fire exit clattered open.

"The main hall is swarming! Down here!"

Three people stumbled into the archive: a university student clutching a bruised shoulder, a terrified young librarian, and the head clerk. They came through coughing, waving their hands at the thin purple mist, their faces pale with terror.

Then they froze.

Through the clearing smoke, they saw the dead, eight-foot scaled monster sprawled across the floor.

And standing squarely on the beast's chest—sniffing its ear with twitching whiskers—was Barnaby.

Barnaby was a fat, one-eared gray cellar rat that had lived behind the history stacks for three years. Arthur occasionally tossed him dry breadcrusts during long night shifts. At this exact moment, Barnaby was simply looking for edible scraps, entirely unbothered by the giant corpse beneath his paws.

Arthur was kneeling behind the counter, sweeping broken glass out of the way and packing his brass wrench into his tool roll.

"Merciful heavens..." the clerk gasped, his eyes bulging. "The monster... it's slain!"

"Look!" the student shouted, pointing a trembling finger at Barnaby. "Look at the beast's chest! That rat... it's standing over its kill!"

"An omen!" the librarian whispered, tears springing to her eyes as she fell to her knees. "An ancient library guardian! The founding spirits have sent a holy beast to protect us! Praise the Guardian of Section 14!"

Barnaby sneezed, found a crumb of dry biscuit on the monster's collar, grabbed it in his paws, and scurried proudly into a gap between the floorboards.

Arthur slowly stood up from behind the workbench, holding a pipe wrench and his canvas kit. A light coating of white lime dust covered his sleeves.

"Hold on," Arthur said, his voice flat and slightly muffled through his mask. "That wasn't the rat. I dumped masonry lime and broke a jug of pipe-cleaning acid. The chemical reaction boiled the air and suffocated—"

"Quiet, boy!" the clerk snapped, waving him off with furious urgency. "Don't blaspheme against our savior with your silly nonsense! Grab your things and show some respect to the guardian spirit! Everyone, through the tunnel before more of them come!"

The three survivors hurried toward the maintenance corridor, whispering prayers of gratitude to the legendary one-eared rat that had supposedly brought down a demon.

Arthur stood alone in the quiet cellar.

Behind his spectacles, his left eye gave a slight twitch.

He looked down at his wrench. He looked at the puddle of steaming acid. He looked at the tiny hole in the wall where Barnaby had disappeared.

"...A rat," Arthur muttered under his breath. "They think an eight-ounce sewer rat took down an eight-foot mutant. Outstanding."

He shook his head, pushed his glasses up his nose, and checked his pocket watch.

Forty-seven hours.

Arthur hoisted his canvas rucksack, verified his spare filters and tools, and kicked open the fire exit leading out to the street.

The sky above the city was already streaked with dark purple clouds. Sirens wailed in the distance. 

Arthur adjusted his mask, gripped his wrench, and stepped into the ruins of the world.
