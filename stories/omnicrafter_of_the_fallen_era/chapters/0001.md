# Chapter 1: The Cracking Stacks

The end of the world started on a Tuesday, and Arthur Vance was mostly annoyed that it interrupted his shelving.

He stood on the fourth rung of a rolling wooden ladder, deep in the chilly sub-basement of the Grand Municipal Library. In his hands was an old, battered guide on mine tunnels and furnace ventilation. It belonged on the top shelf, squeezed between an outdated metallurgy book and a grease-stained manual for steam boilers.

Arthur pushed his glasses up his nose. He was twenty-three, lean, quiet, and wore a faded gray work smock that permanently smelled of cedar shavings and paper glue. To his boss, Arthur was just the slow assistant who spent way too long cleaning the aisles. 

In reality, Arthur was slow because he read everything he touched. Over five years in the basement archives, he had read practically every old manual, engineering journal, and forgotten record in the building. He knew how old timber held up under weight, which cleaning chemicals could melt through copper pipes, and when an odd creak in the floor was something to worry about.

And right now, the floor was doing a lot more than creaking.

*Crack.*

The sound was deep and heavy, rattling right up through the soles of his boots.

A second tremor slammed through the room. Mortar dust rained down from the ceiling arches like gray snow. Across the aisle, a whole row of heavy leather-bound city records tumbled off the shelves, crashing onto the rug.

Arthur didn't panic. Panic just made your heart race and clouded your head. He climbed down the ladder with practiced calm, pulled out his brass pocket watch to time the intervals, and knelt down to lay his hand flat on the stone floor.

The stone was hot. Not warm—hot.

Granite wasn't supposed to heat up like a woodstove. And it definitely wasn't supposed to pulse like a living heartbeat.

Then the stone gave way.

With a shriek like tearing metal, a wide crack ripped straight down the center of the basement floor, tearing open from the emergency exit all the way through Section 14.

Out of the black trench poured a thick, glowing purple mist. It rolled across the floor like heavy liquid, pooling around his boots. The second it touched the room, a strange, sickly-sweet smell filled the air—like rotting flowers mixed with burnt copper.

*The old texts weren't exaggerating,* Arthur realized, his jaw tightening.

Months ago, buried inside a crate of forgotten excavation papers, he had found a torn parchment that spoke of sleeping power deep in the earth, and a cursed purple mist that drove living creatures insane. He had assumed it was an old superstition.

Apparently, it wasn't.

Upstairs, the screaming started.

It began as a chaotic scramble on the main floor—chairs tumbling over, shouting voices, crowds stampeding for the revolving street doors. But within seconds, the nature of the screams changed. The human cries warped into wet, guttural howls, followed by the sickening crunch of breaking wood and splintering bone.

Arthur reached into his smock and pulled out a pocket-sized radio. He twisted the dial through a wall of static until an emergency broadcast broke through:

"—repeating urgent alert from Civil Defense! Strange ruptures reported all across the city! Stay inside and do not go near the purple mist! Biological hazard—I repeat, do not breathe the air—"

Static tore through the signal, and then a panicked dispatcher screamed in the background:

"—Ward 4 at St. Jude Clinic is breached! The quarantine is failing! The fever ward patients are reacting to the air! Their numbers are going crazy, we can't—"

The line cut out with a deafening pop.

Arthur's grip on the radio tightened until his knuckles turned white.

*St. Jude Clinic. Ward 4.*

Four miles west. Right across the busiest part of town.

His fifteen-year-old sister, Eleanor, had been locked in Ward 4 for three years. The doctors had never been able to diagnose her fever. Her skin ran burning hot day after day, and whenever the attacks hit, faint blue lines pulsed beneath her skin like glowing rivers. Every medicine in modern hospitals had failed. Two days ago, the head doctor had quietly told Arthur that Eleanor didn't have much time left.

*She wasn't sick,* Arthur thought, his chest tight. *Her body was waking up to whatever is coming out of the ground.*

If that purple poison filled the hospital, her fever would spike until her body gave out. He had hours—maybe two days at best—to reach her.

"Step one," Arthur murmured aloud, forcing his voice to stay dead calm. "Don't breathe the purple stuff. Step two: get to Eleanor. Step three: put down anything that tries to stop me."

The purple haze was already swirling past his knees.

Arthur spun toward his workbench. He didn't waste a second. On the counter was his usual cleanup gear: jars of crushed charcoal used for drying out damp books, clean linen wraps, and an old brass workshop mask with a twist-off filter cup.

His hands moved fast and steady.

He dumped two big scoops of crushed charcoal into the cup. Charcoal was simple, but nothing beat it for trapping smoke and fumes. He soaked a thick cotton cloth in vinegar to catch the stinging vapor, pressed it over the charcoal, and screwed the brass cap on tight.

He pulled the mask over his face and tugged the leather straps snug behind his ears.

He took a slow breath.

The air pulled through clean and cool. The rotten-flower stink was gone.

*Works like a charm,* he thought.

He tucked the old parchment fragment and his pocket watch into his inner coat pocket, then grabbed his heavy steel pipe wrench from under the bench.

Before he could take two steps toward the exit, the heavy oak door to the upstairs stairwell bowed inward with a deafening crash.

*SLAM.*

The iron latch sheared off. The wood splintered, and something huge dragged itself through the wreckage into the basement.

It used to be the library's security guard. Now, his blue uniform hung in shredded ribbons over a swollen, monstrous frame. His right shoulder had exploded into a mountain of raw muscle covered in thick, rough black scales. His mouth was stretched wide into a snout of jagged teeth, and his eyes were completely milky white, blind and weeping yellow fluid.

The beast lowered its heavy skull, sniffing the cellar air with wet, rattling snorts. 

Arthur stood frozen behind the workbench, holding his breath.

*Blind as a stone,* Arthur thought, his eyes scanning the monster from head to toe. *It's hunting by sound and scent. The shoulder is covered in armor thick as tree bark, but the throat... the throat is pale and totally bare.*

The creature took a heavy, dragging step forward, its claws gouging deep trenches into the stone floor.

Arthur glanced down.

Right by his boot was an open sack of quicklime—the dry, white masonry powder the basement maintenance crew used to stop leaks. And sitting right above it on the shelf was a heavy glass jug of concentrated cleaning acid, used to clear out rusted sewer pipes.

Arthur didn't have magic powers, and he didn't have a magic sword. But he knew what happened when strong acid hit dry masonry lime.

With a quiet flick of his foot, Arthur kicked the sack over. A thick pile of white powder spilled across the stones right in the beast's path.

The shuffle of canvas caught the monster's ear. It let out a bloodcurdling screech and lunged forward, claws raised to swipe.

Arthur hoisted the heavy jug of acid and slammed it straight down into the white pile.

*SMASH.*

The glass burst. The acid flooded into the dry powder.

The reaction was violent and immediate. A roaring cloud of boiling, stinging white steam exploded upward, engulfing the entire front half of the room.

The monster charged right into the blast.

It took a single huge breath of the boiling cloud. The burning fumes hit its raw throat like liquid fire. The beast's roar instantly choked into a wet, suffocated wheeze. It clawed frantically at its own neck, thrashing against the oak counters as its throat swelled shut in seconds.

*THUD.*

The eight-foot monster crashed face-first onto the stones. Its legs kicked twice, twitched, and went completely limp.

Arthur exhaled behind his mask, tapping his wrench against his palm. "Old cleaning supplies. Still undefeated."

Before the white steam had even settled, the rear fire door flew open.

"Down here! The main lobby is full of them!"

Three people tumbled down the steps into the archive: a college student holding a bloody arm, a trembling young librarian, and the chief clerk. They stumbled through the gloom, coughing in the thin fringes of the purple mist, their faces pale with terror.

Then they stopped dead in their tracks.

Through the clearing smoke, they saw the gigantic, scaled nightmare lying motionless on the floor.

And standing right on top of the monster's chest—sniffing its ear with curious, twitching whiskers—was **Barnaby**.

Barnaby was a fat, one-eared gray sewer rat who had lived behind the history stacks for three years. Arthur often tossed him crusts of sourdough bread during quiet graveyard shifts. Right now, Barnaby was just looking for dropped crumbs, totally unbothered by the giant corpse under his paws.

Arthur was kneeling behind the counter, shaking glass shards off his boots.

"Good lord..." the chief clerk whispered, his knees trembling. "The demon... it's dead!"

"Look!" the student screamed, pointing a shaking finger straight at the fat rat. "Look at the beast's chest! That rat... it's standing over its kill!"

"A miracle!" the librarian sobbed, dropping to her knees with her hands clasped. "The founders of the library have sent a guardian spirit to save us! All hail the Sacred Rat of Section 14!"

Barnaby sneezed, spotted a dry cracker crumb near the monster's collar, picked it up with both paws, and trotted happily back into a hole in the baseboard.

Arthur stood up from behind the workbench, holding his pipe wrench, covered head to toe in white lime dust.

"Hey, hold on," Arthur called out, his voice slightly muffled through the mask. "That wasn't the rat. I dumped masonry lime and smashed a jug of pipe acid. The fumes choked it out—"

"Quiet, boy!" the clerk snapped, waving his arms in sheer panic. "Don't insult our holy savior with your nonsense! Grab a broom and clean up that white dust before the guardian spirit returns! Everyone, into the tunnel before more monsters show up!"

The three survivors rushed past him into the maintenance tunnel, loudly chanting praises to the miraculous one-eared rat that had saved their lives.

Arthur stood alone in the quiet basement.

Behind his glasses, his left eyelid gave a slow, deliberate twitch.

He looked at his pipe wrench. He looked at the smoking puddle of acid. He looked at the tiny mouse hole in the corner.

"...A rat," Arthur muttered under his breath. "They really think a fat sewer rat took down an eight-foot monster. Incredible."

He shook his head, tapped his pocket watch, and took a deep breath.

Forty-seven hours left.

Arthur slung his canvas bag over his shoulder, tightened the straps on his mask, and kicked open the fire door leading to the outside world.

The sky above the city was already bruised purple. Emergency sirens wailed in every direction.

Arthur gripped his wrench, adjusted his glasses, and stepped out into the end of the world.
