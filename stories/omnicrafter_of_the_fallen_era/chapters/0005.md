# Chapter 5: The Iron Convoy

The ground floor of St. Jude Clinic was on the verge of panic.

When Arthur carried Eleanor through the stairwell fire-doors into the main triage bay, the tremors were no longer polite vibrations. Every twenty seconds, the terrazzo floor kicked upward with a violent jolt that sent surgical trays clattering across the tiles. Plaster dust drifted down from ceiling seams like gray flurries of snow, settling on the hair and blankets of forty-seven terrified civilians.

Dr. Nicholas Ward took the lead, his gravelly voice cutting through the rising din like a foghorn. "Listen up! The North Wing foundation is failing! Grab the emergency bags, water crates, and portable oxygen units! We are moving to the vehicle bay immediately!"

A younger nurse with smeared mascara stepped forward, clutching a clipboard to her chest with white knuckles. "Doctor Ward, what about the avenue? The radio says the entire commercial sector is overrun with chimeric predators. We can't walk four miles on foot!"

"We aren't walking," Arthur said.

His voice wasn't loud, but the flat, crystalline certainty in his tone made the entire lobby fall silent.

Arthur gently laid Eleanor onto a rolling stainless-steel gurney, immediately wrapping her in two heavy woolen emergency blankets. The silver sulfadiazine cream had set into a dull gray sheen along her forearms, keeping the runaway starlight currents in her veins dormant and grounded. Her breathing was deep, slow, and stable, though her skin still carried the faint, cool scent of ozone.

He turned toward the hospital maintenance supervisor—a burly, bearded man in blue coveralls named Miller who was clutching a heavy iron spud wrench.

"Miller," Arthur said. "Take me to the motor pool. Now."

The hospital motor pool occupied a semi-enclosed loading bay beneath the southern wing, shielded from the falling purple ash by heavy reinforced concrete arches. Inside sat six vehicles: three modern fiberglass ambulances, two heavy commercial diesel box-trucks used for linen and laundry services, and a flatbed municipal utility maintenance rig equipped with an overhead crane boom and an industrial diesel air compressor.

Miller shook his head bitterly as he pointed at the modern ambulances. "The ambulances won't run, son. The electronic engine control modules fried the second that blue lightning storm rolled over the fourth floor. Modern computerized fuel injectors are dead as doornails."

Arthur didn't even glance at the ambulances. He was already walking toward the two linen delivery trucks.

They were International Harvester commercial diesels from the late 1990s—heavy, brutal beasts with cast-iron inline-six engines, mechanical fuel pumps, and zero fragile microchips. Their chassis sat thirty-two inches off the pavement on heavy leaf springs and dual rear wheels.

"Mechanical injection," Arthur said, tapping the flat of his wrench against the front steel bumper of the lead truck. "No sensors. No electronic control unit. An electromagnetic pulse could scorch the paint, but a cast-iron mechanical pump will run on crude kerosene if you ask it politely."

"The air intakes sit four feet off the ground," Miller pointed out, his eyes narrowing as he realized what Arthur was getting at. "The purple mist down in the street depressions will choke the paper air filters within two miles. The silica dust will glaze the cylinder walls like pottery."

"Which is why we modify them," Arthur replied, already pulling his sleeves up to his elbows. "We have sixteen minutes before the subterranean fissure tears through the central boilers. Davis, Miller—fetch the maintenance toolboxes, two rolls of industrial duct tape, and four heavy cotton shop towels soaked in thirty-weight motor oil."

For the next fourteen minutes, Arthur turned the hospital garage into a field armory.

Under his direction, the survivors split into three coordinated squads:

**Squad One (Dr. Ward and the nurses):** Stripped the triage ward of all critical antibiotics, morphine ampoules, surgical suture kits, and five cases of sterile saline. They converted the cavernous cargo bay of the lead linen truck into an improvised mobile intensive care unit. Using four discarded truck tires as shock-absorbing cushions, they suspended Eleanor's steel gurney in the center of the cargo bed, anchoring the frame to the chassis rails with thick copper ground cables clamped to the truck's heavy steel leaf springs.

**Squad Two (Miller and the maintenance crew):** Modified the engine intakes. Arthur unscrewed the plastic intake horns from both diesel trucks, replacing them with four-inch galvanized drainage pipes scavenged from the plumbing shed. He ran the pipes up along the outside of the windshield pillars, terminating the air intakes six feet above the cab roofs—well above the low-hanging thermal boundary where the dense purple mutagens pooled. Over each intake mouth, Arthur secured a double layer of oiled cotton towels. The sticky oil would trap ninety percent of airborne silica particulates before the air ever reached the combustion chambers.

**Squad Three (Arthur and Davis):** Fabricated the breaching plows. 

Arthur took three heavy tubular steel hospital gurneys, unbolted their casters, and used Miller's oxyacetylene cutting torch to sever the headrests. With heavy C-clamps and two-inch grade-eight bolts, they secured the triangular steel frames across the front bumper of the lead truck, creating a sharp, cow-catcher wedge designed to shove abandoned sedans and charging predators aside without puncturing the engine radiator.

On the flatbed utility rig trailing behind, Arthur mounted two twenty-pound industrial dry-chemical fire extinguishers to the headache rack, wiring their discharge valves to a manual pull-cord inside the cab. Filled with monoammonium phosphate, the extinguishers could blast a blinding, suffocating cloud of alkaline powder behind the convoy to choke the olfactory senses of anything attempting to pursue them.

*3:41 PM.*

Arthur tightened the final bolt on the plow assembly with a sharp, satisfying *clack* of his pipe wrench.

"Convoy status," Arthur called out, wiping a streak of black axle grease from his jaw with the back of his smock.

"Forty-eight civilians and seven medical staff loaded," Dr. Ward reported, stepping out of the rear of the lead truck and clicking the heavy barn doors shut. "Every child and wounded patient is secure on blankets in the second box truck. Eleanor is stable, resting comfortably. We have sixty gallons of diesel in the tanks and four spare jerrycans strapped to the flatbed."

Davis climbed into the passenger seat of the lead plow truck, racking the slide of his twelve-gauge shotgun. "Where are we heading, Vance? The main highway is completely choked with abandoned cars from the afternoon rush hour."

Arthur pulled a laminated municipal transit map from his pocket and laid it across the hood of the truck.

"We avoid Commerce Avenue and the Interstate," Arthur said, pointing a grease-stained finger along a thin black line running northwest along the city's perimeter. "The Old Western Rail Spur. The tracks were decommissioned five years ago, but the elevated gravel ballast is thirty feet wide and raised twelve feet above the surrounding streets. No traffic jams. No low-lying mist troughs. It takes us straight past the municipal scrap yards directly to the base of Blackwood Ridge."

"The rail bridge over Blackwood Creek is narrow," Miller warned. "Single track."

"The trucks have seven-foot track widths; the rail ties are eight feet six inches," Arthur replied without hesitation. "As long as we keep our wheels centered on the outer rail shoulders, the tires won't slip. We reach Bunker Zero-Seven in forty-two minutes if we maintain twenty miles per hour."

Before Miller could answer, the concrete ceiling above the loading bay cracked with a deafening *BOOM*.

A chunk of reinforced concrete the size of an office desk crashed down onto the hood of an empty ambulance twenty feet away, crushing the engine compartment into scrap. Mortar dust rained down in choking sheets.

From the central hospital courtyard, the sound of splintering wrought-iron echoed through the bay.

A hunting pack had tracked the scent of blood to the rear gates.

Four grotesque predators leaped over the garden wall and hit the asphalt of the loading ramp. They were smaller than the Vanguard Strider from the main gates, but far more agile—creatures the size of large panthers, with hairless, mottled-purple hide, disproportionately elongated forelimbs ending in four-inch talons, and segmented chitinous plates shielding their spines. Their long, whip-like tails ended in calcified bone spurs that snapped against the pavement with sharp, rhythmic cracks.

*Bone-Tail Prowlers,* Arthur cataloged in three tenths of a second. *High agility, low ballistic resistance. Pack hunters that target running prey from flanking angles.*

The lead prowler hissed, opening an unhinged jaw lined with rows of serrated bone needles, and crouched to spring toward the open doors of the second truck.

"Mount up!" Arthur roared.

He leaped onto the driver-side running board of the lead truck, grabbed the door frame, and hauled himself into the cab behind the heavy steering wheel. Miller slammed the passenger door shut beside him, his spud wrench gripped in both hands.

Arthur rammed the heavy brass key into the ignition and hit the mechanical starter switch.

The starter motor groaned for one heavy revolution: *chug-chug...*

Then the eight-liter diesel roared to life with a deep, volcanic bark that rattled the glass in the garage windows. A puff of black exhaust blasted from the vertical stack, instantly clearing the air beneath the concrete overhang.

Behind them, the second truck and the flatbed utility rig fired up in unison, filling the motor pool with the deafening, thunderous roar of three heavy industrial engines.

The lead Prowler, startled by the roar of the diesels, hesitated for half a second.

Arthur didn't give it another.

He stomped the heavy clutch pedal to the floorboards, shoved the steel shifter into low gear, and dropped his boot flat against the accelerator.

*VROOOOM!*

Twelve tons of steel and iron surged forward down the concrete ramp.

The front triangular plow hit the lead Prowler dead center at twenty miles per hour.

*CRUNCH.*

The heavy steel gurney frame obliterated the creature's ribcage in an instant, smashing its body beneath the front axle and grinding it into the asphalt under four thousand pounds of front-end diesel weight. 

The remaining three beasts shrieked in outrage, flanking the truck to leap at the cab windows. One lunged toward the driver-side door, its talons scraping against the steel door skin with a screech like nails on a chalkboard.

Arthur didn't flinch. He reached down to the floorboards, grabbed a sealed brass canister of pressurized industrial brake cleaner, and jammed the nozzle through the three-inch gap in the cracked driver's window.

He gave the nozzle a hard, two-second burst directly into the beast's eyes.

Chlorinated hydrocarbons hit the unshielded ocular membranes like boiling oil. The creature howled, clawing frantically at its own face, lost its footing on the running board, and tumbled beneath the rear dual wheels of the second truck trailing five paces behind.

"Clear the ramp!" Arthur shouted, wrenching the heavy steering wheel hard to the right.

The convoy blasted out of the hospital garage into the open air of Pine Street.

Behind them, the entire southern wing of St. Jude Clinic finally surrendered to gravity. With a monumental, slow-motion groan of tearing steel, the fourth-floor masonry gave way. The North Wing collapsed inward upon itself, sliding into the boiling subterranean abyss in an immense plume of violet smoke, shattered brick, and billowing dust.

The clock tower toppled last, the stained-glass face of Saint Jude shattering into ten thousand glittering fragments before vanishing forever into the dark.

Arthur didn't look back in the rear-view mirror. His eyes were locked straight ahead, watching the crumbling pavement of 4th Avenue where the road curved toward the elevated embankment of the Old Western Rail Spur.

Above the city, the sky was pitch-black now, illuminated only by jagged forks of violet lightning and the flickering fires of burning storefronts.

Arthur glanced down at his brass pocket watch sitting on the dashboard beside the oil-pressure gauge.

*3:49 PM.*

He gripped the vibrating wheel with steady, calloused hands, shifted the heavy transmission into third gear, and led the iron convoy out of the dying city.
