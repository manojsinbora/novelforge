# Chapter 8: The First Forging

The South Bend lathe cut into the bar of 4140 chromoly steel with a steady, rhythmic hiss that sounded like bacon frying in an iron skillet.

Long, blue-tempered metal curls peeled from the carbide tool post, dropping into the oil pan below in glowing coils. The shop smelled of sulfurous cutting oil, ozone from the fluorescent ballasts, and hot steel.

Arthur Vance adjusted his clear polycarbonate safety goggles, reached out with his left hand, and dialed the cross-slide handwheel two thousandths of an inch inward.

*11:42 PM.*

Down in the residential tunnels of Sub-Level One, the fifty-five survivors were asleep. For the first time in sixteen hours, there were no screams, no suffocating purple clouds, and no floor-shattering subterranean tremors. The thick granite walls of Bunker Zero-Seven swallowed the chaos of the outside world in absolute, tranquil silence.

Arthur, however, had no intention of sleeping. 

Sleep was an inefficient allocation of biological downtime when precision machining tolerances required his direct cognitive supervision.

On the heavy maple workbench to his right lay the dissected components of his afternoon's salvage:

The twenty-millimeter slate-gray dorsal chitin harvested from the Vanguard Strider at St. Jude Clinic.
Twelve eight-inch serrated bone talons extracted from the Bone-Tail Prowlers.
The battered fourteen-inch drop-forged pipe wrench that had carried him across four miles of falling city.
And two high-pressure pneumatic cylinders salvaged from the hospital's mechanical ventilator racks, rated for three thousand PSI working pressure.

Arthur shut off the lathe motor with a flick of his wrist. The chuck spun down to a silent halt. 

He picked up a vernier micrometer, measured the turned steel spindle, and nodded once. "One point one two five inches. Zero point zero zero zero five runout. Acceptable."

The central challenge of post-apocalyptic engineering, as Arthur had calculated during his thirty-minute dinner of canned navy beans, was structural mismatch.

Standard carbon steel was heavy and prone to shear fatigue under explosive kinetic impacts. Pure mutant chitin, while possessing tensile strength exceeding ballistic Kevlar, was an anisotropic organo-silicate biopolymer; if you attempted to drill or bolt it using conventional machine screws, the stress concentrations would cause the crystalline lattice to shatter like tempered glass.

Arthur had solved the problem with five hundred-degree heated mineral oil.

Two hours ago, he had submerged the Strider’s gray chitin plates into a simmering bath of heavy machine oil on the shop’s electric hotplate. At three hundred and fifty degrees Fahrenheit, the organic collagen matrix within the chitin softened, becoming pliant as vulcanized rubber while preserving eighty-five percent of its mineral density. 

While it was soft, Arthur had clamped the chitin over custom steel mandrels, molding the alien armor into contoured, interlocking bracers designed to wrap around human forearms and shins.

Now, it was cool. And it was terrifyingly rigid.

Arthur picked up his drill, fitted a cobalt-carbide end mill, and began machining interlocking dovetail channels along the inner spine of the forearm bracer.

Into these channels, he pressed the machined 4140 chromoly spine. 

Along the exterior forearm ran a miniature pneumatic accumulator—charged to eighteen hundred PSI from the bunker's central compressor—linked to an articulated push-rod linkage. At the wrist terminal sat a hardened tool-steel quick-detach collar, operated by a heavy knurled collar ring.

At the touch of a palm-actuated pressure switch, the pneumatic piston could cycle forward with four hundred pounds of explosive linear thrust, multiplying human tendon leverage by an order of magnitude.

Onto the wrist collar, Arthur mounted the weapon:

He had disassembled the original fourteen-inch pipe wrench, discarded the cracked cast-iron frame, and rebuilt the head around a solid block of forged tool steel. 

The primary jaw was now tipped with three serrated Bone-Tail talons, brazed into recessed sockets with high-silver solder. Along the trailing spine ran a solid-state step-up transformer tied to a compact four-cell nickel-iron capacitor pack strapped beneath the bicep. 

It was no longer an awkward plumber's tool wired to an automobile battery with frayed jumper leads.

It was **The Chimera Striker**—a modular, pneumatic-assisted kinetic weapon system capable of delivering twelve tons of concentrated crushing force or a twenty-five-thousand-volt neural shockwave at five hundred cycles per second.

Arthur slid his right arm into the padded leather sleeve.

The fitted bracer locked around his forearm with a satisfying, airtight *snick-clack*.

The weight was astonishingly balanced: four point two pounds, resting entirely over his center of forearm mass. When he flexed his fingers, the articulated chitin plates slid effortlessly over each other with the whisper of oiled silk.

Arthur closed his fist.

*PSSSHT-CLANG!*

The pneumatic piston fired forward two inches, locking the tool-steel jaw into a rigid, reinforced punching fist with the speed of a striking cobra. The kinetic impulse shook the heavy wooden workbench, rattling the oil cans without straining Arthur's wrist tendons in the slightest.

Arthur released the palm toggle. The piston hissed softly, venting through a sintered bronze muffler as the jaws retracted smoothly into the guard.

He took off his safety glasses, picked up his brass pocket watch from the lathe bed, and clicked the stem.

*5:48 AM.*

"Total assembly duration: six hours, six minutes," Arthur said aloud to the empty workshop. "Pneumatic seal integrity: nominal. Electrical isolation: one hundred percent."

He sat down on the swivel stool, closed his eyes, and allowed his autonomic nervous system forty-five minutes of controlled alpha-wave biological maintenance.

***

At seven-fifteen in the morning, Arthur entered the medical wing carrying a stainless-steel tray holding two tin mugs of hot chickory coffee and a bowl of warm, salted oatmeal porridge.

The hospital survivors were stirring. In the central recreation hall, mothers were combing their children's hair with water from the polished brass fountains, while men organized supply crates under Dr. Ward’s direction. 

In the surgical suite, Eleanor Vance was sitting upright in her hospital bed. 

She was wearing a clean, oversized olive-drab military wool sweater from the bunker’s supply depot. Her dark auburn hair was combed back, and the brilliant, chaotic blue constellations that had threatened to tear her body apart yesterday had settled into delicate, faint sapphire traceries along her collarbone—quiet, beautiful, and completely dormant.

Beside her, Nurse Vera Bennett was checking her blood pressure with an antique mercury sphygmomanometer.

"One-twenty over seventy-eight," Vera announced, looking up with an appreciative grin as Arthur walked in. "She has the circulatory stability of an Olympic runner, Vance. Whatever you did with that grounding wire yesterday rewrote medical textbooks."

"Basic electrical earthing," Arthur said, setting the tray on the rolling bedside table. "Textbooks are merely prone to neglecting the physical sciences in favor of pharmaceutical intervention."

Eleanor rolled her eyes, but a warm smile lit up her face as she pulled the bowl of oatmeal toward her. 

"Good morning to you too, Artie," she said, dipping her spoon into the porridge. "I see you spent the night sleeping like a responsible guardian."

"I engaged in forty-five minutes of deep neurological restorative dormancy," Arthur replied smoothly, leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed over his chest. "Any further duration would have been an unconscionable surrender to indolence."

Eleanor caught sight of his right arm.

Her spoon paused halfway to her mouth. Her eyes widened, taking in the dark, slate-gray chitin plates strapped across his forearm, the gleaming chromoly steel linkages, and the compact pneumatic piston glinting beneath the sleeve of his gray smock.

"Arthur..." Eleanor whispered, her gaze flicking between the bracer and his spectacles. "What in heaven’s name is that on your arm?"

"A bio-mechanical kinetic leverage apparatus," Arthur said. "Designed to reduce musculoskeletal fatigue during heavy mechanical maintenance."

"It has monster teeth on the knuckles, Arthur."

"Diamond-hard calcium phosphate," Arthur corrected without missing a beat. "Highly effective for removing stubborn scale from industrial pipes."

Eleanor opened her mouth to deliver a devastating rebuttal, but the words were cut short by the harsh, rhythmic clatter of an electric buzzer from the ceiling.

*BUZZZZZZ. BUZZZZZZ. BUZZZZZZ.*

In the corridor, Davis’s voice echoed down the concrete hall, rough and breathless with alarm:

"Doctor Ward! Vance! Get to the security post! The seismic sensors on the quarry road just tripped!"

***

The security monitoring post on Sub-Level One was a fortified concrete pillbox recessed into the granite cliff above the main blast doors.

Through three narrow, eight-inch-thick slits of laminated ballistic glass, Arthur, Ward, and Miller looked down at the gravel turnaround six hundred feet below.

A morning breeze was rustling through the dark pine trees. Up here, the sun was bright and cold, casting long shadows across the quarry floor.

Emerging from the pine tree line, three hundred yards from the sealed bunker gates, were three vehicles.

They were not municipal trucks.

They were heavy four-wheel-drive pickup trucks stripped to the chassis and welded with sheets of rusted diamond-plate steel. Across the radiators hung heavy lengths of industrial logging chain; on the bed of the lead truck sat a mounted Soviet-pattern heavy machine gun with a drum magazine, manned by a gunner wearing a spiked football helmet.

Painted across the doors in jagged white primer was the snarling iron wolf emblem:

**Ironfang Cole's Syndicate.**

Behind the lead truck walked a dozen heavily armed men in leather aprons and scavenged tactical vests, carrying scoped rifles and machetes welded from industrial saw blades. 

The vehicles rolled to a halt seventy yards from the thirty-ton steel blast doors, forming a wide, menacing semicircle in the gravel turnaround.

From the passenger seat of the center truck stepped a massive, broad-shouldered man wearing a heavy fleece-lined jacket over a plate carrier. His bare head was shaved bald, and his left cheek was scarred with deep, chemical burn marks. 

"That's Vance Rogan," Miller whispered, his voice trembling as he gripped the concrete sill of the observation slit. "They call him 'The Grip'. He’s Marcus Cole’s blood brother and chief enforcer. If Cole sent Rogan, he wants blood for that bridge."

Below, Rogan unhooked a battery-powered bullhorn from his belt, raised it to his scarred mouth, and clicked the trigger.

His raspy, amplified voice rolled across the granite cliffs with booming, arrogant menace:

"LISTEN UP INSIDE THE ROCK! WE FOLLOWED YOUR DIESEL LEAKS STRAIGHT UP THE MOUNTAIN SPUR! YOU KILLED THREE OF OUR MEN AT THE TRESTLE! YOU SMASHED OUR ROADBLOCK!"

Rogan took two heavy steps forward, spitting into the gravel.

"IRONFANG WANTS THE MAN WITH THE LIGHTNING SPANNER! DELIVER HIM TO THE GATES IN CHAINS! HAND OVER ALL YOUR MEDICINE, YOUR DIESEL RESERVES, AND EVERY UNMUTATED WOMAN IN THAT VAULT! YOU HAVE FIVE MINUTES TO OPEN THE HATCH, OR WE DROP TWO HUNDRED POUNDS OF MINING DYNAMITE DOWN YOUR VENTILATION STACKS AND SMOKE YOU OUT LIKE RATS!"

Inside the observation post, Dr. Ward’s hand dropped to his service revolver. "They have mining explosives, Arthur. If they collapse the ventilation flues on the upper ridge, the scrubbers will choke on the debris within two hours."

"They won't reach the ventilation flues," Arthur said.

His voice was terrifyingly calm. 

He didn't sound angry. He didn't sound frightened. He sounded like a librarian who had just noticed an unsorted volume on the wrong shelf and was preparing to file it correctly.

Arthur reached down, checked the leather straps securing the Chimera Frame to his right arm, and tested the knurled collar ring.

*Click.*

The three-talon steel jaw was seated firm. The pneumatic accumulator read eighteen hundred PSI. The nickel-iron battery cells hummed with twenty-five thousand volts of silent potential.

"Miller," Arthur ordered quietly. "Keep the main blast doors sealed. Do not cycle the hydraulic bypass under any circumstances."

"What are you going to do?" Ward asked, staring at the armored bracer on Arthur’s arm. "Vance, there are fourteen of them! They have a heavy machine gun on that truck bed!"

"A heavy machine gun requires an unobstructed line of sight and twenty-five degrees of traverse," Arthur replied, already walking toward the narrow steel access ladder at the rear of the observation post. "The secondary emergency escape hatch exits onto the granite shelf eighty feet above the quarry floor, directly behind their blind spot."

He paused on the first rung of the ladder, looking back at the two men with cold, steady gray eyes behind his wire-rimmed glasses.

"Watch the quarry," Arthur said softly. "And observe the difference between scavenged brutality and proper mechanical engineering."
