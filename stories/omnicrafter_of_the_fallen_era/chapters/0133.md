# Chapter 133: The Boston Post Road

The coastal margin of southern New England was an intricate labyrinth of drowned river valleys, crystalline metamorphic ridges, and glacial drift plains. When the Wisconsinan glaciation retreated fourteen thousand years ago, it left behind a low, fractured topography scored by torrential glacial meltwater that carved deep estuarine incisions into the bedrock of Long Island Sound. For three centuries of human transit, the lifeblood of this seaboard corridor had been defined by a single continuous overland artery: the Boston Post Road. Established in 1673 as the King’s Best Highway for mounted postal dispatchers between the Dutch settlement of New Amsterdam and the Puritan capital of Massachusetts Bay, the route had evolved into the four-track, electrified Shore Line of the New York, New Haven and Hartford Railroad—the southern spine of the Northeast rail corridor.

Yet the geographic penalty of hugging the tidewater was severe. To reach Boston from New York, any heavy rail line had to span a succession of massive tidal rivers whose broad, muddy estuaries surged with Atlantic tides and resisted conventional bridgework: the Housatonic, the Connecticut, the Thames, and the Mystic.

The Thames was not a shallow river, but a deep, drowned fjord carved by glacial meltwater, where the waters of the Yantic and Shetucket Rivers merged into a tidal channel over fifty feet deep.
