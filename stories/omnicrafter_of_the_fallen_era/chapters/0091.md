# Chapter 91: Crossing the Prairie State

The morning frost on the eastern bank of the Mississippi River glowed with the cold, pale luminescence of mid-winter.

*Day 31 // 6:30 AM.*

Rolling off the eastern approach spans of the historic Eads Bridge, the armored command train *Prometheus* touched the soil of Illinois. The air temperature registered minus eight degrees Fahrenheit on the pilot car’s bimetallic thermometer, with a damp, bone-chilling eastern wind blowing across the frozen floodplains of the American Bottoms.

Arthur Vance sat at the walnut drafting table in the train’s forward observation car, his gold pocket watch open beside a fresh sheaf of heavy drafting vellum. 

With measured strokes of his mechanical pencil, Arthur plotted the alignment of the abandoned pre-Collapse Illinois Central mainline, tracing the three-hundred-mile trajectory north-northeast toward the southern tip of Lake Michigan:

$$\text{Total Route Distance: } D = 286.4 \text{ Statute Miles} \quad (1,512,192 \text{ ft})$$

$$\text{Average Grade: } G \le 0.45\% \quad (\text{Prairie Flatland Profiling})$$

$$\text{Estimated Transit Velocity: } v = 22.5 \text{ mph} \quad (\text{Including Ballast Tamping and Inspection Stops})$$

"Transit duration across the Prairie State: twelve point seven hours of continuous locomotive operation," Arthur murmured aloud, his voice steady beneath his wool muffler. "Provided the drainage culverts through the Kaskaskia and Sangamon river bottoms have not suffered hydraulic blowouts."

Beside the drafting desk, Master Joseph Kovacs was cleaning the burner nozzle of an antique kerosene pressure lantern, his thick gray beard bristling with frost.

"The old coal miners along the river bluffs told us this line carried fifty million tons of southern Illinois bituminous coal a year before the Collapse, Arthur," Kovacs said, checking the wick. "The roadbed is built on ten feet of crushed slag and heavy limestone ballast. But three hundred years without ditch maintenance means the drainage tiles have silted up. When the spring thaws come, half these cornfields turn into five-foot-deep swamps."

"Which is why winter transit is thermodynamically and mechanically advantageous," Arthur replied, rotating his steel drafting triangle. "At minus eight degrees Fahrenheit, the subgrade pore water is completely frozen, elevating the soil modulus of subgrade reaction $k$ from forty pounds per square inch per inch to over four hundred. The frozen mud provides a rigid, unyielding foundation capable of bearing our thirty-two-ton axle loads without ballast pumping."

Eleanor Vance stepped into the compartment, holding two steaming ceramic mugs of chicory tea sweetened with sorghum molasses from the Missouri bottoms.

Around her slender figure, her Mid-Tier Sovereign starlight radiated a gentle, translucent golden field that maintained the observation salon at a comfortable seventy degrees Fahrenheit. Outside the double-glazed safety glass, the bleak winter prairie rolled past in an endless expanse of snow-covered corn stubble, shattered concrete grain silos, and rusting high-voltage electrical towers stripped of their aluminum cables.

"The air feels different here, Arthur," Eleanor said softly, setting a mug beside his ledger. "In the mountains, the wind tasted of pine and raw stone. Across the plains, it smelled of dry grass and dust. But here... there is the bitter scent of old coal, sulfur, and deep, cold water."

"We are approaching the interior drainage basin of the Laurentian Great Lakes," Arthur explained, taking a sip of the warm tea. "The underlying geology consists of the Illinois Basin: deep Pennsylvanian cyclothems containing billions of tons of high-volatile, high-sulfur bituminous coal, bounded to the north by Silurian dolomite and glacial till. For two centuries before the Collapse, this region was the heavy industrial furnace of North America."

Arthur checked his pocket watch.

*6:52 AM.*

"Speed is currently twenty-four miles per hour. We will reach the Kaskaskia River crossing at Milepost Forty-Eight in approximately thirty-five minutes."

The *Prometheus* forged northeast across the frozen prairie with rhythmic, iron majesty.

At the head of the consist, the massive star-iron wedge plow shoved wind-drifted snowbanks off the rails, throwing sparkling plumes of powdered ice forty feet into the gray morning air. Behind the tender, the track maintenance carriage’s high-pressure steam jets blasted ice from the switch frogs, while rotating carbide wire brushes cleared rust scale from the railheads to ensure perfect electrical contact for the transcontinental telegraph line.

At 7:25 AM, the locomotive’s air brakes hissed with a sharp, controlled deceleration as the train slowed to a crawl outside the ruined junction town of Vandalia.

Captain Rachel Cross stepped swiftly onto the forward gangway, holding her field binoculars.

"Arthur, take a look at Bridge Nineteen ahead," Cross called out, pointing toward a five-span through-plate girder bridge spanning the frozen, willow-choked ravine of the Kaskaskia River. "The middle steel pier looks corroded at the waterline. We're picking up significant rust flaking along the splash zone."

Arthur stepped onto the open pilot deck, raising his brass inspection telescope.

Through the optics, Arthur examined the riveted steel bridge pier eighty yards ahead:

"Visual structural assessment: five-span plate girder bridge, vintage nineteen-eighty-four," Arthur dictated to his ledger, clicking his pencil. "Total span: two hundred and fifty feet. The structural steel girders are intact, but Pier Three—located in the historic seasonal flood zone—has suffered severe localized galvanic and microbial corrosion."

Arthur adjusted his focus, noting the reddish-brown tubercles encrusting the structural steel piling:

"Microbially influenced corrosion induced by sulfate-reducing bacteria (*Desulfovibrio*) in the river silt, combined with atmospheric oxygen differential aeration cells," Arthur analyzed. "The structural flange thickness has been reduced from zero point seven five inches to zero point four eight inches across an eight-foot vertical section. Current safety factor under train live loading has degraded to one point two two."

Master Kovacs leaned over the railing, scowling at the pitted steel. "One point two two is too thin for the *Prometheus*, Arthur! If the locomotive’s front driving wheel strikes that span under dynamic impact, the flange could buckle in web crippling!"

"Correct," Arthur nodded. "Which is why we will execute an immediate **In-Situ Cathodic Protection & Auxiliary Web Stiffener Reinforcement**."

The train came to a halt twenty yards before the bridge abutment.

Working under Arthur’s precise engineering instructions, Donald Vance Miller and four machinists of the paroled Civil Labor Corps unloaded forty feet of high-strength star-iron channel iron from the maintenance flatcar, along with four portable magnetic-base drill presses and an electric arc welding set powered by the train's dynamo.

Arthur lowered himself onto the frozen river ice beneath Pier Three, carrying a micrometer caliper, an ultrasonic thickness gauge, and six fifty-pound sacrificial magnesium-aluminum galvanic anode ingots from the *Prometheus*’s metallurgical stores.

"Corrosion mitigation mechanics," Arthur explained to Donald Miller as they positioned the magnetic drills against the corroded web plates:

"Steel corrosion in aqueous environments is an electrochemical reaction: $\text{Fe} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$. By electrically bonding high-purity magnesium-aluminum anodes to the steel piling and sinking the anodes into the saturated river silt, we shift the electrochemical potential of the steel structure to minus zero point eight five volts relative to a copper-copper sulfate reference electrode. The steel becomes the cathode in the galvanic cell, halting all further oxidation."

Miller locked the magnetic drill into place with a crisp mechanical *clack*, smiling in genuine appreciation. "Sacrificial protection! We used that on the hull plates of the Gary ore carriers! The magnesium rots away while the star-iron stays clean as a whistle!"

"Precisely," Arthur said. "And to restore the lost section modulus of the flange, we will fillet-weld four six-by-four-inch star-iron angle stiffeners across the thinned web zone."

*ZZZZZZZZT-SPARK-FLASH!*

Electric arcs flared beneath the frozen bridge trusses, casting brilliant blue and violet light across the snow-covered willows.

Under Kovacs and Miller’s expert hands, four heavy star-iron stiffeners were welded directly over the corroded girder web using low-hydrogen E7018 welding rods. 

Arthur supervised every single weld pass, applying acoustic couplant gel and a five-megahertz shear-wave ultrasonic transducer to inspect the weld roots:

"Ultrasonic flaw inspection: zero slag inclusions, zero lack-of-fusion defects," Arthur announced. "Tensile bond strength verified at seventy-two thousand PSI."

Simultaneously, Arthur drove six magnesium anode spikes through holes augered into the frozen riverbed, bolting heavy copper bonding cables directly to the bridge footing.

Arthur checked the digital voltmeter:

$$\text{Cathodic Protection Potential: } E = -0.92 \text{ Volts (CSE)}$$

$$\text{Section Modulus Restored: } Z = 142.6 \text{ in}^3 \quad (\text{Safety Factor: } 2.45)$$

"Flange reinforcement and cathodic protection completed," Arthur recorded in his ledger with his mechanical pencil. "Safety factor elevated to two point four five under Cooper E-80 loading. The Kaskaskia bridge is certified for full-speed transit."

Arthur climbed back to the deck, brushing frozen gray scale from his greatcoat.

He pulled his gold pocket watch, clicked the hunter case open, and noted the time:

*8:48:15 AM.*

"Total maintenance delay: one hour and twenty-three minutes. Master Kovacs, sound the advance."

Kovacs pulled the whistle cord:

*WHOOOOO-TOOOOO!*

The *Prometheus* rolled smoothly onto the reinforced steel spans. Pier Three absorbed the forty-ton axle loads with rock-solid rigidity, deflecting by less than a millimeter as the heavy train crossed the Kaskaskia River without incident.

As the morning progressed, the train accelerated to its cruising speed of thirty miles per hour, slicing through the snow-bound heart of Illinois.

Towns that had been isolated for three centuries—Effingham, Mattoon, Champaign-Urbana—passed by the observation windows. Along the rail sidings, small survivor communes of prairie farmers and mechanics stood atop snowdrifts, waving lantern banners and cheering as the great silver locomotive thundered past on tracks they had believed dead forever.

Eleanor stood on the rear platform, using her gentle 528 Hz starlight to transmit pulses of warmth and peace toward the settlements, broadcasting on the open shortwave radio channel:

*‘Citizens of the Prairie State... the Western Confederation has restored the transcontinental corridor. The seed vaults are open. The telegraph is alive. Maintain the tracks, and fuel and medicine will follow.’*

At 3:15 PM, the flat farmland began to give way to vast, sprawling industrial marshlands interlaced with frozen canals and black slag embankments.

Ahead on the northern horizon, towering banks of oily yellow-black smoke rose against the sky, punctuated by the dull, rhythmic concussive thuds of heavy drop-forge hammers and distant blast furnace whistles.

Arthur Vance stood on the forward gangway, raising his binoculars toward the smoke.

Through the winter haze, the titanic silhouettes of pre-Collapse steel mills came into view: massive spherical blast furnace stoves, towering coal coke batteries, and colossal gantry cranes lining the frozen turns of the Calumet River. 

"Geographic boundary reached: Lake County, Indiana and Cook County, Illinois," Arthur reported calmly, clicking his mechanical pencil. "Entering the Calumet Industrial Corridor."

Donald Vance Miller stepped beside Arthur, his gaze fixed on the smoky horizon with deep, reverent recognition.

"Blue Island Yards is five miles ahead, Mr. Vance," Miller said, pointing to the sprawling network of tracks unfolding before them. "A hundred and twenty sorting tracks, electropneumatic retarders, and the largest locomotive turntable in the Midwest. If we take Blue Island, the rail throat to Chicago and Gary is wide open."

"Then we take Blue Island," Arthur replied, checking his pocket watch.

*3:42 PM.*

"Prepare the switch-cleaning crews. We enter the Chicago Southern Yards."
