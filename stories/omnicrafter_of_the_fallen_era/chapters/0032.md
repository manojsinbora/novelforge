# Chapter 32: The Transcontinental Mainline

The iron wheels of Depot Shunter Number 7 screamed against the curved manganese steel of the eastern junction switch.

*Day 5 // 9:42 AM.*

Behind the forty-four-ton locomotive, the consist stretched fifty yards along the rock ballast: Car 42, its heavy oak interior reinforced with steel struts and packed with twenty tons of emergency wheat flour; followed by the six-ton flatbed technical truck chained securely to the open deck of Car 18, its tool racks bristling with hydraulic lifting rams, portable oxy-acetylene tanks, and heavy graphite rail-welding molds.

In the right-hand engineer’s seat, Arthur Vance adjusted his clear rubber safety goggles. His gray wool coat was buttoned to the neck against the brisk eastern mountain draft that poured through the open cab windows.

Beside him, Eleanor leaned out the left cab door, her woolen scarf fluttering in the slipstream. Her Starlight Sovereign resonance pulsed with a quiet, rhythmic hum, scanning the miles of steel rail stretching ahead.

"The track ahead is cold, Artie," Eleanor reported, her voice carrying clearly over the deep, syncopated chugging of the dual Caterpillar diesels. "No trains have run through this cut since Tuesday afternoon. But the ground under the rails... it's vibrating. Something has been chewing on the telegraph line."

"Chewing on galvanized iron and copper wire indicates specialized metabolic mineralization," Arthur observed in his quiet, analytical cadence. "Certain subterranean insectoid chimeras consume metallic conductors to harden their chitinous exoskeletons against predator penetration. Westinghouse brake pipe pressure: ninety-two PSI. Main reservoir: one hundred and twelve. Speed: twenty-four miles per hour."

Ahead of them, the landscape transitioned from the wooded pine terraces of Blackwood Ridge into the desolate, vertical defile of the **Razorback Cut**.

Blasted out of sheer metamorphic gneiss during the peak of transcontinental railroad expansion in 1912, the cut was a canyon four miles long and barely forty feet wide, flanked by towering granite walls that rose two hundred vertical feet into the azure sky. 

Running through the floor of the cut was the **Great Northern Transcontinental Mainline**—two tracks of heavy-duty, one-hundred-and-fifteen-pound-per-yard continuous welded steel rail, anchored to creosoted oak ties and deep granite ballast.

At Milepost 22, the mainline came to an abrupt, violent dead end.

Arthur immediately dumped the train air with a practiced, sweeping flick of the automatic brake handle.

*PSSSSSSSSSSSH-CLANK.*

The iron brake shoes clamped down hard against all eight drive wheels. Sparks showered from the trucks as forty-four tons of decelerating steel ground to a smooth, shuddering halt forty yards before the obstruction.

***

Arthur climbed down the cab ladder, his work boots crunching into the crushed granite ballast. Behind him, Captain Cross, Sergeant Cole, Davis, and Miller stepped down from the trailing cars, their M4 carbines held at the low ready.

The scene at Milepost 22 was an engineering catastrophe.

A four-hundred-foot timber and steel trestle bridge spanned a ninety-foot-deep chasm known as **Broken Creek Gorge**. But the bridge was blocked: a sixty-car freight train carrying sulfur and iron ore had derailed during the Day Zero seismic shockwave. 

Three fifty-ton hopper cars lay on their sides, their heavy cast-steel wheel assemblies twisted into scrap. More critically, forty feet of the westbound mainline track had been sheared from its tie plates, twisted into an S-shaped buckle by thermal expansion and the violent lateral impact of the derailed hoppers.

"Look at that rail," Sergeant Cole swore softly, kneeling by the twisted steel. "Bent like a warm stick of black licorice. We can't push through that with our shunter plow; we'd derail the locomotive into the ravine."

Captain Cross stepped to the bridge abutment, peering through her tactical binoculars. "And the bridge itself? Are the timber bents structurally sound?"

Arthur knelt beside the twisted rail, withdrawing a twelve-inch steel combination square and a brass dial thermometer from his coat pocket.

He pressed the thermometer probe against the web of the steel rail.

"Ambient steel temperature: forty-eight degrees Fahrenheit," Arthur announced methodically. "The timber bents and structural steel stringers beneath the deck exhibit zero transverse shear displacement; the bridge structure itself is eighty-eight percent intact. The failure is localized to a forty-foot section of one-hundred-and-fifteen-pound rail."

Arthur looked at his pocket watch.

*10:14 AM.*

"We have three hours and thirty-four minutes before the molten iron in Blast Furnace Number Four cools to critical solidification," Arthur calculated. "A detour around the Razorback Cut would require sixty miles of unmaintained logging spurs and twelve hours of transit time. We will repair the mainline track directly."

Davis blinked in disbelief. "Arthur, that’s four tons of twisted high-carbon rail! We don't have a rail crane or a maintenance crew!"

"We possess fifty tons of hydraulic lifting force, an oxy-acetylene torch, and forty pounds of alumino-thermic welding powder," Arthur replied with calm, mathematical finality. "That is more than sufficient. Miller, Davis—deploy the porta-power hydraulic rams from the flatbed. Sergeant Cole, prepare the thermite cutting torch."

For forty minutes, the gorge echoed with the sounds of intense, calculated industrial labor.

Working under Arthur’s direction, the team went to work:
1. **Severing the Damaged Section:** Using an oxy-acetylene cutting torch operating at forty PSI of oxygen and five PSI of acetylene, Arthur cleanly severed the twisted forty-foot section of damaged rail, slicing through the three-inch steel web with showers of brilliant orange slag.
2. **Replacement Rail Procurement:** From the undamaged eastbound passing siding twenty yards away, Arthur, Cole, and Miller unbolted a clean forty-foot length of unbent 115-pound rail using a heavy four-to-one torque multiplier.
3. **Hydraulic Alignment:** Positioning two twenty-five-ton Simplex hydraulic toe-jacks beneath the replacement rail, Arthur dialed the alignment until the rail heads matched the existing track with less than one-thirty-secondth of an inch of vertical runout.
4. **Thermal Expansion Gap Calculation:** Arthur checked his thermometer again:

$$\Delta L = \alpha \cdot L \cdot \Delta T$$

"Expansion coefficient of high-carbon rail steel: zero point zero zero zero zero zero six five inches per inch per degree Fahrenheit," Arthur noted, adjusting the joint gap with steel feeler gauges. "A gap of precisely one quarter of an inch is required to prevent thermal buckling during midday heating."

With the replacement rail aligned, Arthur clamped two split-graphite **Alumino-Thermic Rail-Welding Crucibles** over the joint.

He was pouring forty pounds of iron oxide and atomized aluminum powder into the crucible when Eleanor suddenly gasped, her hand darting to Arthur’s shoulder.

"Arthur! Up on the cliff walls!"

***

Arthur looked up.

High above the gorge floor, clinging to the sheer, eighty-degree granite walls of the cut, over twenty armored silhouettes were skittering down the rock face.

They were eight feet long, moving on six segmented, barbed legs that clattered against the stone like iron spikes. Their carapaces were not the dull chitin of forest insects; they glistened with a polished, metallic copper-and-zinc luster. In place of soft antennae, two thick, coiled strands of stolen heavy-gauge copper telegraph wire twitched from their triangular heads, arcing with faint blue static discharges.

**Rail-Stalker Mantises.**

"Tier Two Specialized Mineralivorous Chimeras," Arthur diagnosed instantly, stepping between Eleanor and the cliff face. "They feed on creosoted petroleum and copper communications wire. Their carapaces have incorporated metallic copper and galvanized zinc, creating a highly conductive biological shell."

"Cole! Cross!" Arthur called out calmly. "Do not expend lead ammunition against the cliff face; the sixty-degree deflection angle will cause ricochets into our fuel tanks."

Sergeant Cole lowered his M4, his jaw clenched. "Then what the hell do we hit them with, Vance? They’re fifty feet up and closing fast!"

"They have spent forty-eight hours consuming high-conductivity copper," Arthur said, his eyes scanning the electrical conduits running along the side of Depot Shunter Number 7. "They have made themselves walking short-circuits."

Arthur turned to the technical truck's open tool bed.

He grabbed a heavy fifty-foot spool of four-ought copper welding cable, clamping one heavy bronze ground lug directly to the running rail beneath his boots.

He ran the other end into the locomotive cab, where the **General Electric 44-Ton Switcher’s** dynamic braking grid and auxiliary 600-volt DC traction generator bus sat behind a heavy steel access panel.

"Miller! Engage the locomotive’s auxiliary engine at high idle!" Arthur ordered. "Throw the dynamic brake field contactor to position four!"

Miller scrambled into the cab and hauled the field contactor lever back.

*VROOOOOOOOM!*

The twin Caterpillar diesels roared to eighteen hundred RPM, spinning the massive direct-current traction generators to maximum field excitation. The open bus bars hummed with a blistering **Six Hundred Volts DC at Four Hundred Amperes**.

Arthur stepped to the edge of the timber bridge abutment, holding the bare, stripped copper terminal lug in his left hand—the **Sovereign Aegis** buckler—insulated by his silica aerogel gauntlet.

Down from the cliff walls scrambled the swarm of twenty copper-plated mantises, their metallic scythes snapping with hunger as they smelled the hot engine oil and copper windings of the locomotive.

The lead mantis vaulted thirty feet from the rock face, landing directly on the steel running rail ten paces in front of Arthur, its copper antennae crackling with static as it hissed.

Five more mantises landed on the rail behind it, forming a continuous physical bridge of metallic copper-infused legs and carapaces connecting the iron rail to the stone cliff.

Arthur Vance did not flinch.

"Current delivery vector established," Arthur said quietly.

He slammed the live 600-volt, 400-ampere terminal cable directly against the steel running rail.

*BOOOOOOOOOOOOOOM-CRACK!*

A blinding, magnesium-blue flash of direct-current electrical energy surged down the one-hundred-and-fifteen-pound steel rail.

The result was a catastrophic industrial electrocution.

Because the Rail-Stalkers’ carapaces were infused with ingested copper and zinc, their internal electrical resistance was less than two ohms. When six hundred volts of high-amperage direct current tore through the rail, the electricity took the path of least resistance—surging up through their conductive legs, through their copper-plated thoraxes, and arcing into the granite wall.

*BZZZZZZZZZZT-SNAP-BANG!*

The copper antennae on every mantis exploded in showers of green-and-blue electrical sparks.

Their internal fluid compartments flash-boiled into high-pressure steam, detonating their copper-plated carapaces from the inside out with a series of concussive acoustic pops that sounded like string of heavy firecrackers.

All six mantises on the rail seized in violent, fatal tetanic convulsions, smoking black fluid pouring from their joints as they tumbled lifelessly off the bridge into the ninety-foot chasm below.

The remaining fifteen mantises on the cliff wall, sensing the immense electromagnetic shockwave that had vaporized their vanguard, let out terrified, high-pitched mechanical chitters, scrambled back up the granite face, and vanished into the mountain pine ridges.

The gorge fell dead silent, save for the steady, reassuring chug of the locomotive's Caterpillar diesels.

Arthur stepped back from the rail, disconnected the terminal lug with his insulated tongs, and checked his pocket watch.

*11:02 AM.*

"Elapsed combat engagement: eleven point four seconds," Arthur said, without a trace of breathlessness. "Fuel expended: zero point four gallons. Ammunition expended: zero."

He turned back to the alumino-thermic rail-welding crucibles sitting on the joint.

"Now," Arthur said, striking his flint igniter. "Let us finish the weld."

*KER-BLAM!*

A blinding white flare of four-thousand-degree thermite plasma erupted inside the graphite crucible. Four thousand degrees of molten high-carbon iron flooded the rail joint, fusing the forty-foot replacement section into a seamless, unbroken band of continuous welded steel.

Ten minutes later, the graphite molds were knocked away with Arthur’s brass inspection hammer. The joint was ground smooth with a portable angle grinder, and Arthur verified the track gauge:

**Fifty-six and a half inches.** Exactly standard AAR gauge.

Arthur Vance climbed back into the cab of Depot Shunter Number 7, wiped a speck of soot from his spectacles, and grasped the brass throttle lever.

"The Razorback Cut is open," Arthur announced, notching the throttle to **Notch 5**.

The locomotive whistled twice—long and proud—and the iron train surged across the repaired trestle bridge, hurtling east at thirty-five miles per hour toward the smoking chimneys of Vulcan Junction.
