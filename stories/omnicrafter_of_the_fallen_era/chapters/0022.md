# Chapter 22: The Drowned Tunnels

The walls of the Blackwood River Gorge closed in like the jaws of a crushing granite vise.

*Day 3 // 7:42 AM.*

For eight miles north of High Rock, the timber railway had climbed a steady two-point-two percent mountain grade, clinging to a narrow shelf blasted out of solid metamorphic rock. 

Here, eight hundred feet below the snowy ridges of the continental divide, the canyon narrowed to barely sixty yards across. The roar of the river was no longer a background murmur; it was an all-consuming, concussive thunder that made the steel cab of Depot Shunter Number 7 vibrate like the skin of a drum.

At Timber Mile 14, the rails disappeared into the river.

Arthur Vance touched the automatic air brake, bringing the forty-four-ton locomotive to a smooth halt fifty yards before the submerged track end.

Through the salt-spattered windshield, the view was awe-inspiring and terrifying in equal measure.

Rising out of the churning mist, spanning the entire breadth of the canyon from granite wall to granite wall, stood the monolithic face of the **Iron Ridge Hydro-Dam**.

Built in 1952 by the Army Corps of Engineers, the dam was a colossal curved concrete gravity barrier rising two hundred and eighty feet into the sky. It was designed to hold back thirty-five million acre-feet of mountain reservoir water—a massive inland sea impounded between two mountain ranges.

Now, that concrete wall was groaning under catastrophic hydraulic pressure.

All four of the dam's massive, seventy-foot radial spillway gates were completely closed, sealed behind dense, calcified accretions of jagged purple bio-coral that had grown across the concrete sluiceways. 

Because the spillways could not discharge, the reservoir was overtopping.

A continuous, three-foot sheet of wild, foaming white water was spilling over the dam's concrete crest parapet, cascading down the two-hundred-and-eighty-foot curved face in a deafening, vertical avalanche of spray that generated hurricane-force downdrafts of freezing mist.

"Look at that crest!" Miller yelled, cupping his hands around his mouth as he stepped out onto the locomotive running board. "The water is three inches below the top of the roadway parapet! If the lake rises another foot, the dynamic surge will scour the canyon abutments and tear the concrete right off the mountain!"

Arthur pulled his clear rubber safety goggles down over his eyes, adjusted his brass-rimmed respirator, and pulled his leather field notebook from his smock.

"Reservoir surface area: twelve thousand acres," Arthur calculated, his pencil moving smoothly against the wet paper. 

"Inflow rate from sub-alpine snowmelt and subterranean fissure venting: eighty-four thousand cubic feet per second. Discharge rate through blocked penstocks: zero. At current precipitation gradients, structural overtopping failure will occur in precisely five hours and eighteen minutes."

Dr. Ward walked up beside Arthur, shielding his eyes from the blinding spray. "The surface access road on the west cliff is completely buried under a three-hundred-foot rockslide. Arthur, how do we get inside that powerhouse?"

Arthur pointed toward the base of the dam, forty feet below the churning surface of the river.

Emerging from the concrete foundation were four massive, twenty-foot-wide circular concrete tunnels: **The Tailrace Discharge Conduits**.

During normal operation, these conduits carried millions of gallons of spent river water from the interior hydraulic turbines back into the river. But because the turbines were shut down, the water inside the lower tunnels was semi-stagnant, flooded to a depth of four feet.

"The lower tailrace inspection gallery," Arthur said. "It connects the riverbed directly to the Sub-Level 3 turbine maintenance floor. The watertight flood doors were designed to withstand fifty PSI of hydrostatic back-pressure."

"We're wading through four feet of freezing mountain water?" Davis asked, shivering in his wool coat.

"Thirty-eight degrees Fahrenheit," Arthur corrected calmly. "Mild hypothermia will occur in twenty-two minutes without insulated waders. Fortunately, the locomotive’s rear tool locker contains four pairs of vulcanized rubber line-maintenance waders and two heavy waterproof six-volt lanterns."

Ten minutes later, Arthur, Eleanor, Miller, and Davis were suited in heavy chest-high rubber waders, their boots cinched tight with braided nylon cords.

Arthur had checked the waterproof seals on the **Chimera Frame Mark II**. 

Both star-iron bracers were pressure-sealed with silicone grease along their pneumatic piston housings, and the internal nickel-iron capacitor cells were isolated inside airtight rubber gaskets.

Arthur stepped off the river gravel into the flooded mouth of Tailrace Conduit 2.

The water hit his rubber waders like liquid ice, but the current was sluggish, swirling in dark, iridescent turquoise eddies. The concrete tunnel stretched two hundred yards into the dark heart of the mountain, its vaulted ceiling dripping with mineral lime and glistening with delicate, glowing veins of pale blue crystalline algae.

Eleanor walked closely behind Arthur, her hand resting lightly on the trailing edge of his left Star-Chitin buckler.

Her green eyes were alert, reflecting the faint blue luminescence of the water.

"Arthur," Eleanor whispered, her voice tight with tension. "The water isn't empty."

Arthur stopped dead in his tracks.

He raised his hand, signaling Miller and Davis to halt twenty paces back.

Arthur unclipped the heavy six-volt waterproof lantern from his belt, directed the narrow amber beam into the dark water ahead, and watched the surface.

Fifty feet deeper into the flooded tunnel, the water rippled.

Not with the gentle current of mountain runoff, but with the sharp, predatory S-curves of swimming predators.

Breaking the surface of the black pool were three dorsal fins—not the soft cartilage of river trout, but jagged, eight-inch spines of dark, calcified bone dripping with glowing violet slime.

**Gorge Leviathan Stalkers.**

They were Tier 2 Intermediate amphibious chimeras—nine-foot predators combining the muscular, sinuous bodies of giant river moray eels with the armored bone-plates of subterranean crocodilians. 

Their snouts ended in four-inch serrated shears capable of snapping an iron railway tie in half, and along their flanks ran twin rows of bioluminescent electric organs that pulsed with an angry, crackling violet charge through the water.

*BZZZZ-CRACKLE.*

The water around their scales fizzed with dying static sparks, sending ripples of dead minnows floating to the surface.

"Electric eels..." Davis breathed, his shotgun trembling in his hands. "Arthur, if they discharge in this water, we'll be cooked like sausages!"

"Water conductivity: two hundred and forty microsiemens per centimeter," Arthur analyzed instantly, his voice completely calm. "A freshwater medium creates high electrical resistance compared to saline. Their biological discharge relies on concentrated electrolytic field projection across a two-meter radius."

Arthur didn't raise a firearm. Firing a shotgun into four feet of water would merely generate acoustic cavitation and blow back into their own faces.

Arthur stepped forward until the water reached his waist.

He extended his left arm—the **Sovereign Aegis** buckler—plunging the curved Star-Chitin plate six inches below the water's surface.

"Eleanor," Arthur whispered. "Ground the circuit to my left wrist."

Eleanor reached forward, her bare fingers touching the star-iron induction coil on Arthur’s left forearm.

*HUMMMMMMM.*

A circle of pure sapphire starlight flared through the dark water, illuminating the concrete tunnel walls in a halo of brilliant blue light.

The three armored river predators sensed the bio-electric disturbance.

With a furious, hissing thrash of their bone-plated tails, the three leviathans lunged through the flooded tunnel at twenty miles per hour, their jaws gaping wide to reveal rows of needle-sharp ivory teeth.

*Distance: thirty feet. Twenty feet. Ten feet.*

At five paces, the lead predator arched its armored spine, preparing to discharge five thousand volts of bio-electric current straight into Arthur’s chest.

Arthur struck first.

He didn't fire an offensive shock. He depressed the directional phase-inversion toggle on the Sovereign Aegis buckler.

*BZZZZZZZZZZT!*

Thirty-five thousand volts of starlight-amplified current pulsed through the star-chitin plate into the water—not as a chaotic arc, but as a focused, high-frequency square wave tuned precisely to ninety-eight hertz: the resonant frequency of an aquatic predator's lateral line sensory nerve.

To creatures whose entire nervous system was hardwired to hunt through microscopic electrical pulses, Arthur’s thirty-five-thousand-volt resonant shock was the equivalent of a lightning bolt detonating directly inside their inner ears.

The three predators shrieked—a bubbling, strangled wail that echoed through the flooded concrete vault.

Their long, sinuous bodies locked into rigid, involuntary tetanic arches. 

Their electric organs overloaded, blowing out in clouds of harmless white steam, and all three nine-foot beasts flipped belly-up onto the surface of the water, completely paralyzed and comatose.

Arthur lowered his buckler, flicked a drop of cold water from his spectacles, and took a slow breath.

"Lateral-line acoustic sensory overload," Arthur observed, checking his brass pocket watch. 

*8:42 AM.*

"Elapsed engagement duration: one point two seconds. Ammunition expenditure: zero."

He turned back to Miller and Davis, who were staring at the three floating monster carcasses with mouths agape.

"Clear the carcasses to the tunnel wall," Arthur commanded. "We have eighty yards to the watertight powerhouse bulkhead."

They waded past the unconscious beasts, reaching the end of Tailrace Conduit 2.

Carved into the solid eight-foot concrete wall stood a heavy, six-foot watertight submarine door of riveted boiler plate, painted in weathered military gray:

```
U.S. ARMY CORPS OF ENGINEERS // 1952
TAILRACE INSPECTION ACCESS // SUB-LEVEL 3
RESTRICTED PERSONNEL ONLY // SECURE WHEN CLOSED
```

Beside the heavy bronze dog-latch, a small green indicator lamp was flickering faintly beneath a wire cage.

From the other side of the heavy steel door, through eight inches of solid armor plate, came the unmistakable, sharp sound of a military assault rifle charging handle being racked into battery:

*CLACK-CLACK.*

A woman’s voice—sharp, commanding, and taut with exhausted adrenaline—bellowed through the steel intercom grille:

"STOP RIGHT THERE! STEP AWAY FROM THE HATCH AND KEEP YOUR HANDS IN THE AIR, OR WE DEPRESSURIZE THE FLOOD CHAMBER AND DROWN YOU WHERE YOU STAND!"
