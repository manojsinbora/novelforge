# Chapter 22: The Drowned Tunnels

The vertical granite walls of the Blackwood River Gorge closed in like the jaws of an ancient, immovable vise.

*Day 3 // 7:42 AM.*

For eight miles north of High Rock, the single-track timber railway had climbed a grueling two-point-two percent mountain grade, clinging to a narrow shelf blasted out of sheer gneiss and quartz diorite during the timber boom of the 1920s. 

Here, eight hundred feet below the jagged, snow-capped ridges of the northern divide, the canyon narrowed to barely sixty yards across. The roar of the Blackwood River was no longer a distant mountain rumble; it was an all-consuming, concussive thunder that vibrated the structural frame of Depot Shunter Number 7 like the head of a bass drum. The air was frigid, thick with freezing spray and the sharp, mineral tang of wet granite and crushed river gravel.

At Timber Mile 14, the steel rails vanished into the churning river.

Arthur Vance gently applied the Westinghouse independent brake, bringing the forty-four-ton locomotive to a smooth, controlled halt fifty yards before the submerged track end.

Through the salt-spattered, vibrating windshield, the view was awe-inspiring and terrifying in equal measure.

Rising out of the churning veil of mist, spanning the entire breadth of the canyon from granite wall to granite wall, stood the colossal monolithic face of the **Iron Ridge Hydro-Dam**.

Constructed in 1952 by the United States Army Corps of Engineers, the dam was a curved concrete gravity barrier rising two hundred and eighty feet above the canyon floor. Engineered to impound thirty-five million acre-feet of mountain runoff—creating a thirty-mile reservoir lake between the northern ridges—it was an iconic monument to mid-century industrial resilience.

Now, that massive wall of concrete was groaning under catastrophic hydrostatic overload.

All four of the dam's seventy-foot radial Tainter spillway gates were completely jammed shut, sealed behind dense, calcified accretions of jagged purple bio-coral that had metastasized across the concrete sluiceways since Day Zero.

Because the spillways could not vent the incoming mountain torrent, the reservoir was overtopping.

A continuous, three-foot-thick sheet of wild, foaming white water was spilling over the dam's concrete crest parapet, cascading down the two-hundred-and-eighty-foot curved face in a deafening, vertical avalanche of spray. The plunging cataract generated hurricane-force downdrafts of freezing mist that whipped through the canyon, coating the nearby boulders in treacherous black ice.

"Look at that crest!" Miller yelled, cupping his hands around his mouth as he stepped onto the locomotive's steel running board. "The water is less than three inches below the top of the roadway parapet! If the reservoir rises another foot, the dynamic surge will scour the canyon abutments and shear the concrete right off the mountain!"

Arthur pulled his clear rubber safety goggles down over his eyes, adjusted his wool cap, and drew his leather-bound field notebook from his waistcoat pocket.

"Reservoir surface area: twelve thousand four hundred acres," Arthur calculated aloud, his pencil moving with crisp, mechanical strokes against the greaseproof vellum. 

"Inflow rate from sub-alpine snowmelt and subterranean fissure venting: eighty-four thousand cubic feet per second. Discharge rate through blocked penstocks and choked sluices: zero. Hydrostatic head: two hundred and seventy-eight feet, generating one hundred and twenty PSI of baseline pressure at the foundation toe. At current precipitation gradients, structural overtopping failure will occur in precisely five hours and eighteen minutes."

Dr. Nicholas Ward walked up beside Arthur on the running board, shielding his eyes from the blinding spray. "The surface access road on the western cliff is completely obliterated under a three-hundred-foot rockslide. Arthur, how do we get inside that concrete monolith?"

Arthur pointed toward the base of the dam, forty feet below the churning surface of the river.

Emerging from the concrete foundation monolith were four massive, twenty-foot-wide circular concrete flumes: **The Tailrace Discharge Conduits**.

During normal operation, these colossal tunnels carried millions of gallons of spent river water from the interior hydraulic turbines back into the riverbed. But because the power plant had tripped offline on Night Zero, the water inside the lower tailrace conduits was semi-stagnant, flooded to a depth of roughly four feet.

"The lower tailrace inspection gallery," Arthur said, his voice cutting through the roar of the waterfall. "It connects the riverbed directly to the Sub-Level Three turbine maintenance floor. The watertight flood doors were engineered to withstand fifty PSI of hydrostatic back-pressure."

"We're wading through four feet of freezing mountain water?" Davis asked, stepping down from the cab while shivering in his damp wool coat.

"Thirty-eight degrees Fahrenheit," Arthur corrected calmly. "Without thermal insulation, severe peripheral vasoconstriction and mild hypothermia will occur in twenty-two minutes. Fortunately, the locomotive’s rear tool locker contains four pairs of vulcanized industrial rubber line-maintenance waders, talcum powder for seal preservation, and two heavy waterproof six-volt lanterns."

Ten minutes later, Arthur, Eleanor, Miller, and Davis were suited in heavy chest-high rubber waders, their boots cinched tight with braided nylon cords around their waists.

Before stepping into the freezing water, Arthur performed a meticulous pre-dive audit on the **Chimera Frame Mark II**. 

He inspected the vulcanized Buna-N seals on both star-iron bracers, applied a generous coating of Dow Corning 111 silicone grease to the pneumatic piston glands, and verified that the internal thirty-five-thousand-volt nickel-iron capacitor cells were isolated inside airtight rubber gaskets.

Arthur stepped off the river gravel into the flooded mouth of Tailrace Conduit 2.

The water struck his rubber waders with bone-chilling pressure, but the current was sluggish, swirling in dark, iridescent turquoise eddies. The concrete tunnel stretched two hundred yards into the dark heart of the mountain, its vaulted ceiling dripping with mineral lime stalactites and glistening with delicate, glowing veins of pale blue crystalline algae.

Eleanor walked closely behind Arthur, her hand resting lightly on the trailing edge of his left Star-Chitin buckler.

Her green eyes were wide and alert, reflecting the faint blue luminescence of the submerged algae.

"Arthur," Eleanor whispered, her voice tightening with sudden dread. "The water... it isn't empty."

Arthur stopped dead in his tracks.

He raised his right hand, signaling Miller and Davis to halt twenty paces back.

Arthur unclipped the heavy six-volt waterproof lantern from his belt, directed the narrow amber beam into the dark water ahead, and watched the surface.

Fifty feet deeper into the flooded tunnel, the water rippled.

Not with the gentle current of mountain runoff, but with the sharp, predatory S-curves of swimming predators.

Breaking the surface of the black pool were three dorsal fins—not the soft cartilage of river trout, but jagged, eight-inch spines of dark, calcified bone dripping with glowing violet slime.

**Gorge Leviathan Stalkers.**

They were Tier 2 Intermediate amphibious chimeras—nine-foot predators combining the muscular, sinuous bodies of giant river moray eels with the armored bone-plates of subterranean crocodilians. 

Their snouts ended in four-inch serrated shears capable of snapping an iron railway tie in half, and along their flanks ran twin rows of bioluminescent electric organs that pulsed with an angry, crackling violet charge through the water.

*BZZZZ-CRACKLE.*

The water around their scales fizzed with dying static sparks, sending ripples of dead minnows floating to the surface.

"Electric eels..." Davis breathed, his shotgun trembling in his hands. "Arthur, if they discharge in this water, we'll be cooked like sausages!"

"Water conductivity: two hundred and forty microsiemens per centimeter," Arthur analyzed instantly, his voice completely calm. "A freshwater medium creates high electrical resistance compared to saline. Their biological discharge relies on concentrated electrolytic field projection across a two-meter radius."

Arthur didn't raise a firearm. Firing a shotgun into four feet of water would merely generate acoustic cavitation and blow back into their own faces.

Arthur stepped forward until the water reached his waist.

He extended his left arm—the **Sovereign Aegis** buckler—plunging the curved Star-Chitin plate six inches below the water's surface.

"Eleanor," Arthur whispered. "Ground the circuit to my left wrist."

Eleanor reached forward, her bare fingers touching the star-iron induction coil on Arthur’s left forearm.

*HUMMMMMMM.*

A circle of pure sapphire starlight flared through the dark water, illuminating the concrete tunnel walls in a halo of brilliant blue light.

The three armored river predators sensed the bio-electric disturbance.

With a furious, hissing thrash of their bone-plated tails, the three leviathans lunged through the flooded tunnel at twenty miles per hour, their jaws gaping wide to reveal rows of needle-sharp ivory teeth.

*Distance: thirty feet. Twenty feet. Ten feet.*

At five paces, the lead predator arched its armored spine, preparing to discharge five thousand volts of bio-electric current straight into Arthur’s chest.

Arthur struck first.

He didn't fire an offensive shock. He depressed the directional phase-inversion toggle on the Sovereign Aegis buckler.

*BZZZZZZZZZZT!*

Thirty-five thousand volts of starlight-amplified current pulsed through the star-chitin plate into the water—not as a chaotic arc, but as a focused, high-frequency square wave tuned precisely to ninety-eight hertz: the resonant frequency of an aquatic predator's lateral line sensory nerve.

To creatures whose entire nervous system was hardwired to hunt through microscopic electrical pulses, Arthur’s thirty-five-thousand-volt resonant shock was the equivalent of a lightning bolt detonating directly inside their inner ears.

The three predators shrieked—a bubbling, strangled wail that echoed through the flooded concrete vault.

Their long, sinuous bodies locked into rigid, involuntary tetanic arches. 

Their electric organs overloaded, blowing out in clouds of harmless white steam, and all three nine-foot beasts flipped belly-up onto the surface of the water, completely paralyzed and comatose.

Arthur lowered his buckler, flicked a drop of cold water from his spectacles, and took a slow breath.

"Lateral-line acoustic sensory overload," Arthur observed, checking his brass pocket watch. 

*8:42 AM.*

"Elapsed engagement duration: one point two seconds. Ammunition expenditure: zero."

He turned back to Miller and Davis, who were staring at the three floating monster carcasses with mouths agape.

"Clear the carcasses to the tunnel wall," Arthur commanded. "We have eighty yards to the watertight powerhouse bulkhead."

They waded past the unconscious beasts, reaching the end of Tailrace Conduit 2.

Carved into the solid eight-foot concrete wall stood a heavy, six-foot watertight submarine door of riveted boiler plate, painted in weathered military gray:

```
U.S. ARMY CORPS OF ENGINEERS // 1952
TAILRACE INSPECTION ACCESS // SUB-LEVEL 3
RESTRICTED PERSONNEL ONLY // SECURE WHEN CLOSED
```

Arthur did not raise his hands in panicked surrender, nor did he flinch from the iron speaker grille. He stepped squarely in front of Eleanor, planting his vulcanized rubber boots firmly on the concrete threshold, and leaned toward the perforated brass intercom plate.

"Stand down, Captain Cross," Arthur spoke into the grille, his cadence level, polite, and unhurried. "This is Arthur Vance, Chief Archivist and Mechanical Engineer of the Blackwood Ridge Sanctuary, accompanied by Eleanor Vance and logistical personnel. We have arrived via Yard Engine Number Seven with nineteen metric tons of winter wheat, medical supplies, and forty pounds of calcium hydroxide neutralizer. We are here to inspect your generator turbines and clear the blocked spillway sluices."

Inside the heavy steel door, the sharp mechanical click of an assault rifle safety being flicked on was audible through the iron plate, followed by a breathless, incredulous silence that hung heavy in the cold, mist-filled concrete chamber.
