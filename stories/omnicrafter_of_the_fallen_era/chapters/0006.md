# Chapter 6: The Rail Spur Gauntlet

The elevated gravel ballast of the Old Western Rail Spur cut through the industrial district like the spine of a submerged sea serpent.

On either side of the raised embankment, twelve feet below, the low-lying warehouse alleys and storm drainage basins were completely inundated with purple miasma. It pooled across the asphalt like cold liquid nitrogen, swallowing loading docks, rusted shipping containers, and abandoned forklifts in an opaque, simmering lavender broth.

Up on the rail bed, however, the air was relatively clear. 

A stiff westerly wind was blowing down from the Blackwood foothills, pushing the corrosive vapors eastward toward the ruined city center. The three diesel trucks maintained a steady, rumbling pace of twenty-two miles per hour, their heavy dual rear tires straddling the outer edges of the weathered wooden ties with a rhythmic, hypnotic *clack-clack, clack-clack*.

In the driver’s seat of the lead plow truck, Arthur Vance kept his hands relaxed on the lower rim of the vibrating steering wheel. 

His eyes swept across the dashboard gauges every thirty seconds:
*Oil pressure:* forty-eight PSI. Steady.  
*Coolant temperature:* one hundred and eighty-four degrees Fahrenheit. Well within thermal operating tolerances.  
*Intake manifold vacuum:* normal. The oiled cotton towels strapped across the galvanized snorkel pipes were doing their job, trapping the abrasive silica dust before it could score the cast-iron cylinder walls.

Beside him in the passenger seat, Davis was leaning forward against the dashboard, clutching his twelve-gauge shotgun until his knuckles showed white through his skin.

"Look at that down there," Davis muttered, pointing a trembling finger through the side window toward a sprawling scrap metal recycling yard below the embankment. "There's hundreds of them."

Through the thin purple haze, shapes were moving among the towering pyramids of crushed automobiles. 

They weren't animals. They were human beings in various stages of violent, uncontrolled mutagenic transition. Some dragged elongated, chitin-plated arms along the gravel; others crouched on the roofs of rusted boxcars, their jaws clicking with the needle-fanged chatter of blind predators. Every time the diesel engines rumbled overhead, dozens of pale, sightless faces turned upward toward the embankment.

"Subterranean miasma targets the limbic system first," Arthur observed calmly, without taking his eyes off the tracks ahead. "Higher executive function shuts down within twenty minutes of unshielded inhalation. What remains is pure reptilian territorial aggression. Keep your window rolled up to two inches. The glass will repel thrown ballast stone, but an acoustic screech can rupture an eardrum at thirty paces."

From the jump-seat behind them, through the small sliding glass hatch that connected the cab to the cargo box, Dr. Ward’s voice called through:

"Arthur! Eleanor’s vitals are holding. Pulse sixty-eight. The copper grounding cables are running warm to the touch, but the chassis is bleeding off the static without sparking. How much farther to the ridge?"

Arthur clicked open his brass pocket watch sitting on the instrument binnacle.

*4:08 PM.*

"Two point two miles to the Blackwood Creek trestle bridge," Arthur replied. "Once we cross the canal, the rail line terminates at the old municipal quarry at the foot of the ridge. From there, it is eight hundred yards up the access road to Bunker Zero-Seven."

"The bridge is coming up around the bend," Miller said, leaning over from the middle seat, his heavy iron spud wrench resting between his knees. "I worked the rail repair crew out here ten years ago. It’s an open-deck steel girder bridge, eighty yards across, fifty feet above the canal."

He frowned, squinting through the mud-spattered windshield as the tracks curved around a massive concrete grain silo.

"Wait a minute," Miller growled, his voice tightening. "What in hell is that on the trestle?"

Arthur’s eyes narrowed behind his wire-rimmed spectacles.

Half a mile ahead, where the rail embankment met the steel girders of the Blackwood Creek bridge, the tracks were blocked.

A rusted fifty-ton steel boxcar had been intentionally derailed across the rails at a forty-five-degree angle, its heavy trucks wedged into the ballast to form an impenetrable barricade. Flanking the boxcar on both sides were walls of welded rebar, corrugated sheet metal, and stacks of heavy industrial steel drums painted with red hazard triangles.

And standing atop the barricade were men.

There were seven of them, silhouetted against the bruised violet afternoon sky. They weren't shambling mindless mutants; they were survivors who had retained their wits while willingly embracing the corruption. 

They wore welder's aprons of scorched leather over heavy denim, their faces concealed behind makeshift steel scrap masks with narrow viewing slits. Their bare forearms were heavily thickened with patches of black keratin scales—the early mark of intentional, controlled miasma inoculation. In their hands they held a lethal assortment of scavenged weaponry: scoped hunting rifles, pneumatic nail guns modified to fire hardened steel spikes, and long iron pipes capped with crude commercial dynamite sticks.

Across the front of the derailed boxcar, painted in dripping white industrial primer, was a jagged emblem: a snarling iron wolf with crossed rail spikes.

"Ironfang Cole's scrap syndicate," Miller spat, his face draining of color. "They ran the illegal chop shops down in the rail basin. Ruthless bastards. They must have taken the bridge the second the sirens started."

On the barricade, one of the enforcers stepped forward onto the roof of the derailed car, raised a military flare gun, and fired.

*THWUMP.*

A blinding red phosphorus flare arced through the sky, plunging into the gravel thirty yards in front of Arthur's truck, hissing furiously as it burned through the wooden ties.

From the barricade, an amplified voice bellowed through an industrial megaphone—rough, grating, and dripping with malicious amusement:

"KILL THE ENGINES, CONVOY! DROP YOUR WEAPONS AND STEP OUT ONTO THE GRAVEL WITH YOUR HANDS ON YOUR HEADS! ALL FUEL, MEDICINE, AND WOMEN BELONG TO IRONFANG COLE! ANYONE TOUCHES A GEAR SHIFTER, AND WE BLOW THE TRESTLE TO KINDLING!"

Davis’s breath hitched in his throat. "Arthur... they've got dynamite rigged to the fuel drums. If we ram that boxcar at speed, the whole front of the truck goes up in a fireball. We can't turn around on an eight-foot embankment!"

"Turning around is an inefficient allocation of diesel," Arthur said.

His voice was dead level, devoid of fear, devoid of hesitation. His mind was already breaking the roadblock down into vectors, masses, and chemical equations.

"Miller," Arthur ordered, his eyes tracking the layout of the bridge. "Check the switch stand fifty yards before the barricade. Do you see the red lantern target?"

Miller squinted through the windshield. "Yeah! The maintenance siding! It leads to the old timber spur, but it’s a dead end—it drops twenty feet off the embankment into the swamp!"

"The switch isn't thrown," Arthur said briskly. "The main line rails run straight under the center of that derailed boxcar. Look at the boxcar’s rear wheel assembly."

Arthur pointed with the handle of his pipe wrench.

"The rear truck of the boxcar is resting on the outside rail crown, held only by a rusted four-inch kingpin and friction against the ballast. The center of mass is tilted twelve degrees toward the open water of the canal. It isn't a solid barrier; it’s an unbalanced cantilever."

"And the explosive drums?" Davis asked, his hands trembling on the shotgun. "They’ll shoot the drums the second we get within fifty yards!"

"They can't shoot what they cannot see," Arthur said. "Davis, grab the manual release cord for the headache rack on the flatbed. When I say pull, yank it until the latch clicks."

Arthur reached down to the floorboards, retrieved his twelve-foot length of heavy copper grounding cable, and stripped the insulation from both ends with his pocketknife. 

With swift, practiced motions, he jammed one end of the copper cable directly into the positive terminal of the truck’s massive dual 12-volt commercial starter batteries beneath the floorboard inspection plate. The other end he clamped securely to the external capacitor terminal of *The Electric Spanner*.

"Twelve volts at six hundred cold-cranking amperes," Arthur muttered, calculating the circuit load. "Fed directly into the step-up transformer. Capacitor saturation in four seconds. Output: twenty-two thousand volts at peak discharge."

*WHINE.*

The small capacitor on the side of the wrench began to scream with a high-pitched, ozone-laden whine, its indicator diode glowing from dull amber to an angry, blinding electric violet.

"Vance!" Ward yelled from the back hatch. "They're raising rifles!"

"Brace for impact!" Arthur shouted.

He slammed his left foot onto the clutch, wrenched the heavy steel floor-shifter into fourth gear, and floored the accelerator pedal to the floorboards.

*ROAAAAR!*

The eight-liter International Harvester diesel howled like an enraged beast. Twin jets of thick black smoke erupted from the vertical exhaust stacks, and twelve tons of armored steel surged forward along the rails, accelerating from twenty to thirty-five miles per hour in seconds.

On the barricade, the Ironfang gunners panicked.

"THEY'RE NOT STOPPING! FIRE! BLOW THE DRUMS!"

Two rifle shots cracked out: *BANG-BANG.*

The bullets struck the heavy triangular hospital gurney plow welded to the truck's front bumper, ricocheting harmlessly into the gravel with sharp, metallic pings.

"NOW, DAVIS! PULL!" Arthur roared.

Davis yanked the braided steel cord with both hands.

Behind them on the flatbed utility rig, the valves of both industrial fire extinguishers snapped wide open.

*FSSSSSSSSSSSH!*

Forty pounds of pressurized monoammonium phosphate powder erupted into the air, creating a massive, rolling wall of blinding, chalk-white chemical fog fifty feet wide and twenty feet high. Driven by the truck’s forward draft, the dense white cloud rolled over the bridge like an avalanche, completely engulfing the roadblock.

The enforcers on the boxcar coughed violently, blinded by the burning alkaline dust, their rifle sights completely obscured by the wall of white smoke.

Through the fog, Arthur locked his eyes on the red explosive drums lashed to the barricade.

*Distance: twenty-five yards.*  
*Speed: thirty-eight miles per hour.*

Arthur kicked open the driver's door three inches, leaned out into the slipstream, and hurled *The Electric Spanner* like a javelin straight through the smoke.

The tool didn't hit the men.

The sharp steel chisel bit deep into the thin sheet metal of the lead red fuel drum, puncturing the pressurized casing.

Simultaneously, the trailing copper grounding wire snapped taut against the truck’s front bumper.

*SNAP-CRACKLE-BOOM!*

Twenty-two thousand volts of raw electrical potential bridged across the punctured drum in an instantaneous, blinding electrical arc.

The fuel vapors detonated.

A massive fireball of orange flame and black smoke erupted across the right side of the barricade, blowing the corrugated metal shields and the three syndicate gunners backward into the air like ragdolls. The blast tore away the wooden ties securing the boxcar’s front axle, leaving the fifty-ton car teetering on a single rusty wheel set above the open abyss of the canal.

One second later, Arthur's truck arrived.

*KABOOOOM.*

The heavy triangular gurney plow struck the bottom sill of the teetering boxcar at thirty-eight miles per hour with the combined kinetic momentum of twelve tons of roaring diesel steel.

The impact rattled Arthur's teeth in his skull. The front leaf springs groaned in agonizing compression, and the steering wheel violently whipped against his palms.

Arthur didn't let go. He locked his elbows, braced his boots against the floorboards, and kept the accelerator pinned flat against the metal.

Under the irresistible force of the wedge plow, the boxcar’s rusted kingpin sheared clean off with a deafening screech of tearing cast iron.

The fifty-ton steel boxcar tipped sideways over the edge of the trestle.

For half a second, it hung suspended in the air. Then, with a monumental splash that sent a sixty-foot wall of spray into the sky, the railcar plummeted fifty feet down into the rushing waters of Blackwood Creek, smashing into the rocky riverbed below.

The convoy blasted through the shattered remnants of the roadblock in a shower of splintered wood, smoking metal fragments, and swirling white chemical powder.

Behind them, the four surviving Ironfang enforcers crawled out of the gravel on hands and knees, their scorched leather aprons shredded, staring through wide, terrified eyes at the iron juggernaut that had just demolished their fortified bridge in five seconds flat.

To men who had spent their lives believing in brute force and scrap-metal supremacy, what they had just witnessed was impossible.

A machine wrapped in lightning and white ghost-smoke had materialized out of nowhere, summoned a thunderbolt that detonated their explosives, pulverized a fifty-ton train car with a steel wedge, and vanished into the foothills without slowing down for a single heartbeat.

"The... the Ghost..." one enforcer whispered through blistered lips, clutching a shattered arm as he stared into the swirling smoke. "He summoned the storm... Ironfang said nothing could cross the trestle..."

Within hours, that whispered terror would reach the ears of Marcus Cole himself—the first dark legend of **The Ghost Artificer**, an unstoppable phantom who waged war with celestial lightning and iron.

Arthur, however, was currently busy checking his tire pressures.

"Davis," Arthur said calmly through his respirator as the truck rolled off the steel bridge onto the solid granite bedrock of Blackwood Ridge. "The steering has a slight pull to the left. The impact knocked the front toe-alignment out by approximately three degrees. Remind me to adjust the tie-rod sleeve once we reach the gate."

Davis let out a long, ragged breath, looked at the empty shotgun in his hands, and slowly began to laugh—a wild, hysterical laugh of pure, unadulterated relief.

"You got it, Vance," Davis wheezed, wiping sweat and white fire-extinguisher powder from his forehead. "Adjust the tie-rod. Top priority."

Ahead of them, the landscape transformed.

The elevated tracks came to an end in an abandoned granite quarry surrounded by dense, towering stands of dark lodgepole pines. Up here, six hundred feet above the city basin, the air was crisp, clean, and completely free of the purple miasma. The scent of pine resin and wet mountain stone washed through the truck's open vents like cold spring water.

Cut directly into the sheer vertical face of the granite cliff stood the entrance to **Blackwood Ridge: Bunker Zero-Seven**.

Two twelve-foot-high, thirty-ton blast doors of interlocking reinforced steel stood embedded in the bedrock, flanked by heavy concrete pillboxes and weathered air intake stacks dating back to the height of the Cold War.

Arthur eased off the accelerator, touched the air brakes with a gentle hiss, and brought the iron convoy to a smooth, perfect halt in the gravel turnaround before the gates.

He reached into his waistcoat, clicked open his brass pocket watch, and nodded.

*4:24 PM.*

Forty-six minutes elapsed from St. Jude Clinic. Zero civilian casualties. Vehicle functionality: ninety-four percent.

Arthur turned off the diesel ignition, letting the massive engine tick quietly as it cooled in the mountain air.

"Right," Arthur said, reaching into the footwell to retrieve his spare wrench and slinging his rucksack over his shoulder. "Step one: secure the airlocks. Step two: get Eleanor into a sterile isolation berth. Step three: find the bunker's machine shop."

He kicked open the cab door, stepped down onto the clean mountain gravel, and looked up at the great steel doors of their new home.
